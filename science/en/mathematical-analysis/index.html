Mathematical Analysis & Differential Equations

Limits and continuity

Analysis makes approximation precise. A sequence converges when its terms eventually remain within every prescribed tolerance of a limit. A function is continuous at a point when nearby inputs produce nearby outputs. Pointwise convergence controls each point separately; uniform convergence controls a whole domain with one bound. Exchanging a limit with a derivative or integral requires hypotheses, such as dominated convergence for integrals.

Derivatives and integrals

A derivative measures local linear change. Integration accumulates a function relative to length, volume, or a more general measure. The fundamental theorem of calculus connects differentiation and integration under suitable regularity. Multivariable analysis uses gradients, divergence, and differential operators; integration by parts transfers derivatives and exposes boundary contributions.

Evolution and boundary data

An ordinary differential equation (ODE) involves derivatives with respect to one independent variable; a partial differential equation (PDE) involves several. An initial-value problem specifies a starting state, while a boundary-value problem specifies conditions on a spatial boundary. Linear equations obey superposition for homogeneous solutions; nonlinear equations generally do not. Local Lipschitz dependence on the state gives local uniqueness for many ODEs, but does not guarantee global existence: solutions may blow up in finite time.

Elliptic, parabolic, and hyperbolic equations

The principal part classifies many second-order linear PDEs. The Laplace equation \(\Delta u=0\) is elliptic and typically describes a boundary-controlled equilibrium. The heat equation \(\partial_t u=\kappa\Delta u\), \(\kappa>0\), is parabolic and smooths initial data in standard settings. The wave equation \(\partial_t^2u=c^2\Delta u\) is hyperbolic and propagates signals at finite speed. Systems and nonlinear equations need more careful classifications; these examples are not an exhaustive taxonomy.

Fourier analysis

Fourier analysis decomposes functions into frequency components. On \(\mathbb R^d\), with kernel \(e^{-ix\cdot\xi}\), differentiation becomes multiplication by \(i\xi\) when the transform is defined appropriately. This turns constant-coefficient linear PDEs into algebraic or simpler evolution problems in frequency space. Boundary geometry, nonlinear products, and limited regularity can prevent a simple mode-by-mode solution.

Function spaces

A normed space assigns a size to vectors or functions. A Banach space is complete in its norm: Cauchy sequences converge within it. A Hilbert space is complete in a norm induced by an inner product, providing orthogonality and projection. For \(1\leq p<\infty\), \(L^p\) identifies functions equal almost everywhere and uses \(\|u\|_p=(\int|u|^p)^{1/p}\); \(L^\infty\) uses the essential supremum. \(L^2\) is Hilbert. The chosen space determines what convergence and stability mean.

Weak derivatives and distributions

A distribution is a continuous linear functional on smooth compactly supported test functions. It includes locally integrable functions and objects such as the Dirac delta. Distributional differentiation is defined by integration by parts. A weak derivative is represented by a locally integrable function satisfying that identity. Sobolev spaces \(W^{k,p}\) control weak derivatives through order \(k\); \(H^k=W^{k,2}\). For example, \(|x|\) has weak first derivative equal to the sign function despite lacking a classical derivative at zero.

Weak solutions, compactness, and regularity

A weak solution satisfies a tested integral equation rather than requiring every derivative pointwise. Weak convergence controls pairings with dual test objects; it is weaker than norm convergence. A priori bounds can yield convergent subsequences through compactness, but passing nonlinear terms to the limit often requires stronger convergence. Sobolev embeddings trade derivative control for integrability or continuity under hypotheses involving dimension, exponent, and domain. Compact embeddings require additional restrictions; bounded sets in an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space are not generally norm compact. Regularity asks whether a weak solution has additional differentiability. Existence of weak solutions alone does not imply smoothness or uniqueness.

Well-posedness and scientific use

A problem is well posed in a specified class if a solution exists, is unique, and depends continuously on the data in the chosen topology. Numerical stability and consistency must be assessed in relation to that problem; a stable-looking animation is not a convergence proof. Navier-Stokes uses energy bounds, weak compactness, and regularity estimates. Quantum mechanics uses Hilbert-space operators with specified domains; QFT uses distributions and regulated products. Relativity combines geometric equations with constraints and gauge choices. Numerical simulation approximates these structures and must separate discretization error from model error.
