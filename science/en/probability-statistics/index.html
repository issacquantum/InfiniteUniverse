Probability & Statistics

Probability spaces

A probability model consists of a sample space \(\Omega\), a sigma-algebra \(\mathcal F\) of measurable events, and a probability measure \(P\). An event is a measurable set of outcomes. Kolmogorov’s axioms require nonnegativity, normalization, and countable additivity for pairwise disjoint events. Mutually exclusive events cannot occur together; they need not be independent.

Zero probability and almost sure events

The empty event is impossible and has probability zero, but the converse fails. Under a uniform distribution on \([0,1]\), each particular point has probability zero although a point is sampled. A property holds almost surely when its exception set has probability zero. “Almost surely” does not mean “for every outcome.” Countable additivity does not extend to arbitrary uncountable sums.

Conditioning, Bayes, and independence

For \(P(B)>0\), conditioning restricts the reference population to \(B\). Bayes’ theorem reverses the conditioning by combining a likelihood with a prior probability. Independence means \(P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)\); conditional independence is a separate property and can change when information is added. Conditioning on a zero-probability observation requires conditional densities or regular conditional distributions, not division by zero.

Random variables and distributions

A random variable is a measurable function of the outcome. A probability mass function (PMF) assigns probabilities to discrete values. For an absolutely continuous law, a probability density function (PDF) gives probabilities through integrals; its value at a point is not a probability and may exceed one. The cumulative distribution function \(F_X(x)=P(X\leq x)\) applies to discrete, continuous, and mixed laws. Not every continuous distribution has a density.

Expectation and dependence

Expectation averages with respect to a probability measure, provided the relevant integral exists. Variance measures squared dispersion around the mean. Covariance measures joint centered variation, and correlation normalizes covariance by the standard deviations when both are positive and finite. Zero correlation does not generally imply independence. Heavy-tailed laws may have undefined means or infinite variances.

Discrete and continuous examples

Bernoulli models a binary trial with success probability \(p\). Binomial counts successes in \(n\) independent Bernoulli trials with the same \(p\): \(P(X=k)=\binom nk p^k(1-p)^{n-k}\). Poisson counts have \(P(X=k)=e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k/k!\), with \(\lambda>0\), and describe counts in a fixed interval under a homogeneous Poisson process. A Gaussian density is \(f(x)=e^{-(x-\mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)}/(\sigma\sqrt{2\pi})\), \(\sigma>0\). Exponential waiting times have density \(\lambda e^{-\lambda x}\) for \(x\geq0\), and zero otherwise; they are memoryless. These are models whose assumptions require checking, not universal descriptions of data.

Large samples

For independent identically distributed variables with finite absolute mean, the strong law of large numbers gives almost sure convergence of the sample mean to the expectation. The classical central limit theorem additionally assumes finite, nonzero variance: \(\sqrt n(\bar X_n-\mu)/\sigma\) converges in distribution to a standard Gaussian. The data themselves need not become Gaussian. Dependence and heavy tails require other hypotheses or different limit laws.

Inference and uncertainty

A likelihood treats observed data as fixed and varies a model parameter; it is not generally a probability distribution over parameters. Estimation chooses a parameter value or distribution from data and assumptions. A frequentist confidence interval is a procedure with a specified repeated-sampling coverage under the model, not a posterior probability for a fixed parameter. A Bayesian credible interval instead has stated posterior probability conditional on a prior and likelihood. A p-value is a tail probability under the null model, not the probability that the null is true. Testing also involves power, effect size, multiple comparisons, and model adequacy. Frequentist and Bayesian interpretations answer different questions; neither is a universally accepted philosophy of all probability. Epistemic uncertainty concerns incomplete knowledge, while aleatory uncertainty describes variation within a chosen model; the distinction depends on what is modeled.

Processes and Monte Carlo

A stochastic process is a family of random variables indexed by time or another parameter. The Markov property says that the conditional future depends on the present state rather than the full past, for the chosen state description. Monte Carlo estimates integrals through sampling; independent finite-variance sampling typically gives an error scale proportional to \(n^{-1/2}\). Markov-chain Monte Carlo uses dependent samples and needs convergence and mixing checks; many iterations alone do not establish accuracy.

Classical and quantum probability

In a normalized pure state, a rank-one projective measurement gives \(P(a)=|\langle a|\psi\rangle|^2\). More generally \(P(a)=\operatorname{Tr}(\rho E_a)\), where \(\rho\) is positive with unit trace, and the positive measurement effects sum to the identity. An amplitude is a complex number, not a probability. Indistinguishable alternatives add amplitudes before squaring: \(|\alpha+\beta|^2=|\alpha|^2+|\beta|^2+2\operatorname{Re}(\alpha^*\beta)\). The cross term permits interference. Each fixed quantum measurement yields ordinary normalized probabilities; the structural difference lies in noncommuting observables and the impossibility, in general, of representing all measurement contexts by a single noncontextual classical joint model. This does not select an interpretation of quantum mechanics.

Connections across science

Statistical mechanics assigns ensembles to microscopic states; information theory quantifies uncertainty and coding limits. Biology uses stochastic inheritance and population models, while machine learning estimates patterns and predictive uncertainty. Simulation makes these models computational, and quantum mechanics supplies measurement probabilities. In every case the sampling mechanism, dependence, and model assumptions matter.

Probability axioms

$$P(\Omega)=1,\quad P(A)\geq0,\quad P\!\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}A_n\right)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}P(A_n)$$

Conditional probability and Bayes

$$P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}=\frac{P(B\mid A)P(A)}{P(B)}$$

Expectation and variance

$$\mu=\mathbb E[X]=\int_\Omega X\,dP,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(X)=\mathbb E[(X-\mu)^2]$$
