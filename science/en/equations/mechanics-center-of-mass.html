Center of Mass

Center of Mass

Equation

$$\mathbf R_{\mathrm{CM}}=\frac{\sum_i m_i\mathbf r_i}{M_{\mathrm{tot}}},\quad M_{\mathrm{tot}}=\sum_i m_i$$

Literal Reading

“Vector R subscript CM equals the sum over i of m subscript i times vector r subscript i, divided by M subscript tot. M subscript tot equals the sum over i of m subscript i.” Sum the entire numerator before dividing.

Common Reading

The center of mass is the average position weighted by mass. A heavier part counts more in locating that point.

How a Physicist Interprets It

Choose the particles or parts of a body and express their positions relative to one origin, at the same instant. Their masses and positions determine the center of mass. This point can lie outside the material, as it does at the center of a uniform ring. Its location does not, by itself, specify the body’s rotation or shape.

Symbols

  • \(\mathbf R_{\mathrm{CM}}\): center-of-mass position vector, in metres. CM labels the center of mass; it is not a product of C and M.
  • \(\mathbf r_i\): position vector of particle i, in metres. Bold type marks vectors; an index identifies which particle is meant.
  • \(m_i\): scalar mass of particle i, in kilograms. \(M_{\mathrm{tot}}\): their total mass in kilograms; “tot” means total.
  • \(i\): dimensionless particle label, running over all particles in the chosen system. Here it is not the imaginary unit.
  • \(\sum_i\): add the following term for every particle. \(m_i\mathbf r_i\) multiplies a scalar by a vector and has units kg m. Dividing the vector sum by total mass gives metres. The two equals signs state definitions; the comma separates them.

Values and Example

There is no universal constant. Masses and positions are measured or specified as model inputs. On a line, put 1 kg at \(x=0\) m and 3 kg at \(x=4\) m. Total mass is 4 kg, and \(X_{\mathrm{CM}}=(1\cdot0+3\cdot4)/4=3\) m. Read this as “X sub CM equals one times zero plus three times four, all divided by four, equals three metres.” Here x and X are scalar positions on the chosen axis; each numerator product has units kg m. The center lies closer to the heavier mass.

Meaning of the Equation

First add the masses. Next multiply each position by its mass, add these vectors, and divide by total mass. The same operation is applied to each spatial component. Translating the origin shifts all positions and the center by the same amount; it does not change their relative geometry.

$$M_{\mathrm{tot}}\ddot{\mathbf R}_{\mathrm{CM}}=\mathbf F_{\mathrm{ext}}$$

Read the derived relation as “total mass times the second time derivative of the center-of-mass position vector equals the net external force vector.” Two dots denote two time derivatives, so \(\ddot{\mathbf R}_{\mathrm{CM}}\) is acceleration in m/s²; \(\mathbf F_{\mathrm{ext}}\) is the vector sum of external forces in N. Differentiate the definition twice for constant masses, multiply by total mass, and apply Newton’s second law to each particle. Internal forces cancel in pairs when Newton’s third-law condition holds. The remainder is the external resultant.

Scope and Limits

The instantaneous mass-weighted definition requires a finite, nonzero total mass, but does not require the masses to remain constant over time. The derived force equation additionally assumes constant particle masses, an inertial frame and cancellation of internal forces in Newtonian mechanics. A system exchanging matter needs the momentum carried across its boundary; its force balance cannot be inferred from the constant-mass derivation. Relativistic energy and field momentum require a different treatment.
