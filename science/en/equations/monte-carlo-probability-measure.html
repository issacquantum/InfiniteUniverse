Monte Carlo Sampling

Monte Carlo Sampling

Equation

$$\mu = \mathbb{E}_p[f(X)] = \int f(x)\,p(x)\,dx$$
$$\hat{\mu}_N = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N} f(X_k)$$
$$X_k \sim p(x)$$

Literal Reading

“Mu equals the expectation, under p, of f of X, equals the integral of f of x times p of x with respect to x.” Then: “Mu-hat subscript N equals one over N times the sum, from k equals one to N, of f of X subscript k.” Finally: “X subscript k is distributed according to p.” The tilde denotes a distribution here, not approximation.

Common Reading

The exact average over a probability distribution can be estimated by averaging the values obtained from random samples drawn from that distribution.

How a Physicist Interprets It

Choose a normalized sampling distribution and a quantity to measure on each sample. The integral is the target expectation; the finite sum is an estimate. Sampling coordinates can visualize an orbital, while averaging an indicator of a region estimates the probability of finding the particle there. The probability law and the sampling algorithm are different things.

Symbols

  • \(\mu\): scalar expectation; \(\hat\mu_N\): scalar estimate from N samples. Both have the units of the function f. The hat denotes estimation, not a quantum operator here.
  • \(\mathbb E_p\): expectation with respect to distribution p. Square brackets enclose the quantity averaged; the subscript names the distribution.
  • \(X\): random variable; \(x\): a possible value and integration variable. \(p(x)\): nonnegative probability density, with reciprocal units to the integration measure so that \(p(x)\,dx\) is dimensionless.
  • \(f\): function evaluated on a sample. Its units depend on the observable; it could be energy in J or a dimensionless region indicator.
  • \(\int\) and \(dx\): integration over the full support of the distribution, with the appropriate volume measure if x has several components. No finite bounds are written in this compact form.
  • \(N\): positive integer sample count; \(k\): integer trial index. The summation includes trials 1 through N, and division by N forms the arithmetic mean. \(X_k\) is sample k. Independent, identically distributed draws are assumed for simple Monte Carlo.

Values and Example

There are no universal physical constants in these three relations. For a dimensionless example, sample uniformly between zero and one and let f return its input. The exact mean is one half. Illustrative draws 0.2, 0.4, 0.8 and 0.6 sum to 2.0; dividing by four gives 0.5. This chosen sample happens to match the expectation. Another sample need not, and adding a sample does not guarantee that the error decreases.

Meaning of the Equation

Evaluate the function once per draw, add the results, and divide by the number of draws. Under independent sampling and an integrable function, the law of large numbers gives convergence to the expectation. A finite-variance assumption is additionally needed for the usual inverse-square-root standard-error formula. A wrong sampling distribution produces the wrong average, however many samples are used.

Orbital Probability Measure

$$dP = |\psi_{320}|^2\, r^2 \sin\theta\, dr\, d\theta\, d\phi$$

Read this expression as “d P equals the squared modulus of psi subscript three two zero, times r squared times sine theta, times d r d theta d phi.” It specifies probability in a volume element, not a sample average.

  • \(P\): dimensionless position probability; \(dP\): its infinitesimal contribution. \(\psi_{320}\): hydrogen orbital with principal quantum number 3, orbital angular-momentum number 2 and magnetic quantum number 0. The three subscripts are labels, not the number three hundred twenty as an exponent.
  • \(|\psi_{320}|^2\): complex modulus squared, the position probability density. With SI coordinates it has units m⁻³. Vertical bars indicate modulus; the superscript 2 squares it.
  • \(r\): radial distance, in m with SI coordinates. \(\theta\): polar angle from 0 to pi; \(\phi\): azimuth from 0 to two pi. Angles are in radians, dimensionless. \(\sin\) is the sine function.
  • \(r^2\sin\theta\,dr\,d\theta\,d\phi\): spherical volume element in m³. The differentials specify integration variables. Multiplying by the density gives dimensionless probability.

The orbital formulas in the source article use atomic units: their radial coordinate is measured in Bohr radii and is a dimensionless numerical coordinate. Their wavefunction must be interpreted in the matching atomic-unit normalization, not as an SI density inserted unchanged. Integrating the normalized density times its volume element over all space gives probability one. For a finite region, integrate over that region; a sample fraction there estimates that probability. Sampling r and both angles uniformly would omit both the orbital density and the spherical volume factor.

Scope and Limits

Simple averaging assumes samples from the stated distribution. Correlated samples require a correlation-aware error estimate, and importance sampling requires weights. The hydrogen orbital measure is a separate application of the Born rule in the nonrelativistic Coulomb model, not a classical electron orbit. The equation gives no trajectory or time sequence to the plotted points.
