Spacetime Interval

Spacetime Interval

Equation

$$ds^2 = \eta_{\mu\nu}\,dx^\mu dx^\nu$$

Literal Reading

d s squared equals eta subscript mu nu times d x superscript mu times d x superscript nu, summed over both repeated spacetime indices.

Common Reading

The spacetime interval stays invariant between reference frames.

Symbols

\(ds^2\): infinitesimal spacetime interval.

\(\eta_{\mu\nu}\): Minkowski metric tensor.

\(dx^\mu\) and \(dx^\nu\): infinitesimal coordinate differences.

\(\mu,\nu\): spacetime indices summed over by convention.

Meaning of the Equation

This page uses the metric signature \((-+++)\): timelike intervals have \(ds^2<0\), lightlike intervals have \(ds^2=0\), and spacelike intervals have \(ds^2>0\).

Conditions and units

In inertial Cartesian coordinates, take \(x^0=ct\), \(x^1=x\), \(x^2=y\), and \(x^3=z\), all with length units, and \(\eta=\operatorname{diag}(-1,1,1,1)\). Then \(ds^2=-c^2dt^2+dx^2+dy^2+dz^2\), in m², with \(\mu,\nu\) each ranging from 0 to 3. Superscripts label coordinate components, not powers. The metric is dimensionless. This is Minkowski spacetime; curved spacetime requires \(g_{\mu\nu}(x)\) instead.

Worked example or interpretation

For cdt=5 m, dx=3 m and zero transverse displacements, the interval is −16 m². Any Lorentz-transformed inertial coordinates give the same value. Purely spatial separation at equal time instead has positive interval in this convention.

Source

Sean M. Carroll, Lecture Notes on General Relativity.
