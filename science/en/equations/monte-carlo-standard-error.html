Monte Carlo Standard Error

Monte Carlo Standard Error

Equation

$$SE(\hat{\mu}_N)=\frac{\sigma_f}{\sqrt{N}}$$

Literal Reading

“The standard error of mu-hat subscript N equals sigma subscript f divided by the square root of N.” Parentheses identify the estimator whose uncertainty is being described.

Common Reading

For independent samples with the same distribution and finite variance, the uncertainty of their average is the single-sample standard deviation divided by the square root of the sample count.

How a Physicist Interprets It

This quantifies variation across repeated independent runs of a sampling calculation. It is not the known difference between one computed answer and the true value, and it does not include errors in the physical model or bias in the sampling method.

Symbols

  • \(SE\): standard error, the standard deviation of the estimator over repeated runs. It has the units of the quantity being averaged.
  • \(\hat\mu_N\): sample mean based on N values of a function f. Mu names the target mean; the hat denotes its estimate, and the subscript is the sample count.
  • \(\sigma_f\): population standard deviation of the values of f under the sampling distribution. It has the same units as f; the subscript identifies the function, not multiplication.
  • \(N\): positive integer sample count, dimensionless. The radical takes its positive square root; the fraction divides the standard deviation by that number. The equality is exact for independent, identically distributed samples with finite variance.

Values and Example

There is no universal constant. The population standard deviation must come from a known distribution or be estimated from sample data. If independent energy samples have a population standard deviation of 3 J, averaging 100 samples gives a standard error of 0.3 J; averaging 400 gives 0.15 J. Halving the standard error therefore needs four times as many samples. Substituting a sample standard deviation gives an estimated standard error, not a newly exact population value.

Meaning of the Equation

Independent sample variances add. For N samples, the sum has N times the single-sample variance. Forming a mean divides every sample sum by N, so its variance is divided by N squared. The remaining variance is the single-sample variance divided by N; taking a square root gives the displayed equation. This reasoning also explains why the square root appears.

Scope and Limits

Independence, an unchanged sampling distribution and finite variance are essential to this formula. Correlation contributes covariance terms, so raw sample count alone is insufficient for a Markov chain. Infinite-variance distributions do not have this finite standard error. A confidence interval requires additional distributional assumptions or a justified large-sample approximation; one standard error is not a guaranteed bound.
