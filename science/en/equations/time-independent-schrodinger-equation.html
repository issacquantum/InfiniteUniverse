Time-Independent Schrödinger Equation

Time-Independent Schrödinger Equation

Equation

$$\hat{H}\psi(\mathbf{r}) = E\psi(\mathbf{r})$$

Literal Reading

“H-hat acting on psi at vector r equals E times psi at vector r.” The hat marks an operator, bold r is a position vector, and parentheses give the wavefunction’s argument.

Common Reading

An energy eigenstate is a state on which the Hamiltonian acts simply by multiplying it by one energy value. This equation finds such states and energies; it does not describe an arbitrary evolving state.

How a Physicist Interprets It

Specify a time-independent Hamiltonian, its domain and boundary conditions, then solve for nonzero eigenfunctions and their energy eigenvalues. Bound-state wavefunctions must be normalizable. The spectrum may also contain continuous energies; confinement and the potential determine which case occurs. Initial preparation is needed to decide which states are occupied, not to define the spectrum.

Symbols

  • \(\hat H\): Hamiltonian operator, with units J. It acts on the entire function that follows it.
  • \(\psi(\mathbf r)\): spatial energy eigenfunction. For a bound, single-particle state normalized over three-dimensional volume, it has units \(\mathrm{m}^{-3/2}\). Scattering eigenfunctions use a different, generalized normalization.
  • \(\mathbf r\): position vector in metres, measured in the chosen coordinate system.
  • \(E\): real scalar energy eigenvalue in J, not an operator and not a universal constant. The equality holds as an equality of functions over the domain. Both sides have units of energy times wavefunction.

Values and Example

For an illustrative two-level system, \(\hat H=\mathrm{diag}(0,\Delta)\) with \(\Delta=2\) electronvolts. “Diag” specifies a diagonal matrix with the listed entries; the off-diagonal entries are zero. In this abstract basis the normalized column vectors \((1,0)^{T}\) and \((0,1)^{T}\) have energies 0 and 2 electronvolts. The superscript T transposes a row into a column; the entries are dimensionless amplitudes. Multiplying the matrix by either column verifies the eigenvalue equation. This matrix example has the same eigenvalue structure as the displayed position-space equation but is not a three-dimensional particle wavefunction. One electronvolt is exactly \(1.602176634\times10^{-19}\) J, so the chosen gap is \(3.204353268\times10^{-19}\) J. The gap is a model input, not a constant of nature.

Meaning of the Equation

For an energy eigenfunction, the Hamiltonian changes its scale but not its shape. The associated time dependence is a complex phase with angular rate energy divided by the reduced Planck constant. Its position probability density is stationary. A superposition of states with different energies generally acquires different phases and need not have a stationary density. Solving this eigenvalue problem is therefore distinct from evolving an arbitrary initial state.

Scope and Limits

A stationary-state construction requires a time-independent Hamiltonian with a physically appropriate self-adjoint domain. A general state need not have definite energy. Discrete energies are not guaranteed: a free particle on unbounded space has a continuous spectrum. The position notation here is for a single spinless particle; other systems require the corresponding coordinates or internal components.
