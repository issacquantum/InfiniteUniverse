First Stars

Formation of the First Stars (Population III Stars)

The first stars, often referred to as Population III stars, began to form approximately \( 1 \times 10^{8} \text{ to } 2.5 \times 10^{8} \text{ years} \). Unlike later generations of stars, Population III stars were composed almost entirely of the primordial elements created during Big Bang nucleosynthesis: hydrogen and helium, with only trace amounts of lithium. There were no heavier elements (which astronomers call "metals") available to facilitate cooling during their formation.

The formation of these first stars required immense gravitational collapse in the densest regions of the nascent universe. Without heavy elements to efficiently radiate away heat, the primordial gas clouds needed to be significantly more massive than the clouds that form modern stars to overcome the higher temperatures and pressures. Theoretical models suggest these first stars were therefore typically very massive, ranging from tens to hundreds of times the mass of our Sun.

Characteristics of Population III Stars

Due to their enormous mass and nearly pure hydrogen-helium composition, Population III stars are expected to have been extremely hot, exceptionally luminous, and very short-lived. Their intense gravitational compression would have driven core temperatures far above those of many later stars, while their luminosities may have reached millions of times that of the Sun. Because of their mass, they likely survived for only a few million years before ending in violent stellar deaths. Their ultraviolet radiation was also crucial for reionization, helping transform the neutral intergalactic medium and bringing the cosmic Dark Ages to an end.

These stars were the universe's first "heavy element factories." Through nuclear fusion in their cores, they synthesized heavier elements such as carbon, oxygen, and iron. When these massive stars reached the end of their short lives, some exploded as powerful supernovae, while others could collapse directly into black holes, depending on mass and stellar evolution. These explosions dispersed the newly formed heavy elements into the surrounding intergalactic medium, enriching it for the formation of subsequent generations of stars and galaxies.

Impact on Cosmic Evolution

The formation and death of the first stars marked a profound turning point in cosmic history. Their radiation helped end the Dark Ages by reionizing neutral hydrogen; their supernova explosions created and dispersed the first heavy elements; and their remnants, together with the enriched gas they left behind, supplied the conditions needed for the earliest stages of galaxy formation.

Primordial, nearly metal-free Population III stars are expected from models of early star formation. Candidate observations and indirect chemical evidence must be distinguished from an unambiguous identification of an individual first-generation star. The James Webb Space Telescope helps test these predictions in early galaxies; inferred stellar masses and lifetimes depend on the formation model.
