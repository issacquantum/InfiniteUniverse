Foundations and Physical Quantities

What counts as a star?

A star is a self-gravitating object whose ordinary stellar life includes sustained hydrogen fusion in its interior. This describes a physical history, not a requirement that fusion occur at every moment or everywhere inside it. A protostar is still forming; an evolved giant may have exhausted hydrogen in its core while burning it in a shell. A star is not a solid ball with a flame on its surface.

A brown dwarf cannot sustain ordinary hydrogen burning for a main-sequence lifetime. It can shine through contraction and cooling, and some brown dwarfs briefly burn deuterium. The dividing mass depends on composition and the physical definition used; one numerical cutoff does not explain every borderline object. Planets can also radiate internal heat. Their classification involves formation context as well as mass, so brightness alone cannot distinguish a planet from a brown dwarf.

White dwarfs, neutron stars, and black holes are stellar remnants, not ordinary hydrogen-burning stars. A white dwarf can be hot and luminous while cooling. A black hole has no material photosphere like the Sun; light from an accretion disk is produced outside its event horizon. Keeping the object and the source of its observed radiation separate prevents misleading comparisons.

Matter, pressure, and energy

Density is mass per volume; temperature describes a thermal state, not the total amount of heat stored. A large cool region can contain more thermal energy than a tiny hot region. Pressure is normal force per unit area, measured in pascals (newtons per square metre). In a momentum-transfer description, it is normal momentum transferred per unit area per unit time. Gravity accelerates matter inward; differences in pressure between neighboring layers can oppose that acceleration. Equal pressure on opposite faces produces no net force.

Much of a star is plasma, matter with freely moving charged particles. Ionization depends on both temperature and density; cool atmospheres can retain neutral atoms and molecules. Radiation carries energy and momentum. Its pressure is important in some stellar interiors, but gas, radiation, and quantum degeneracy make different contributions. Mechanical support and the energy that a star radiates are related questions, not interchangeable ones.

The working units here are metres, kilograms, seconds, kelvins, pascals, and watts. A watt is a joule per second. Solar mass, radius, and luminosity are convenient reference scales, not assumptions that every star resembles the Sun. A radial profile tells how a quantity changes from center to atmosphere, rather than assigning one temperature or density to the whole star.

Mass, composition, and evidence

More mass usually requires higher internal pressures and temperatures, changing reaction rates and the amount of energy that must escape. More fuel does not automatically mean a longer life: massive main-sequence stars use fuel much faster. Initial hydrogen and helium fractions affect particle numbers and available fuel. Astronomers call elements heavier than helium metals; their abundance influences opacity, cooling, nuclear reactions, and winds. This usage includes oxygen and carbon, not just everyday metals.

Telescopes record photons, arrival times, positions, and spectra after instrumental calibration. Surface temperature is inferred from a spectrum or color and an atmosphere model; core temperature is inferred from an interior model. Masses from well-characterized binaries provide a relatively direct dynamical test, while ages usually require evolutionary interpretation. An error bar should include measurement uncertainty and relevant model assumptions.

A useful learning order is mechanics and gravity, thermodynamics, radiation and atomic spectra, then stellar structure. The chapters develop these connections without assuming that a photograph reveals the interior. Agreement among luminosity, radius, binary dynamics, spectra, and oscillations is stronger than fitting a single observable.
