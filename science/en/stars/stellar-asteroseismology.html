Asteroseismology

What oscillates and what reaches a telescope

Derek Buzasi is my main personal influence in asteroseismology. His work is an important reference for how I approach this subject. The field has a much broader history and depends on many researchers, instruments, and collaborations. His contributions belong within that history.

A star's fluid can oscillate around its mean structure. Displacement changes pressure, density, temperature, and the velocity of the visible layers. Telescopes usually measure tiny brightness variations or shifts of spectral lines caused by line-of-sight surface motion. They do not record sound crossing the vacuum between the star and Earth. Audio demonstrations shift measured frequencies into an audible range and are representations of the data.

A light curve records measured flux over time. Calibration removes known instrumental effects, but detrending can also remove real long-period variability. Convection produces fluctuating granulation; spots, flares, eclipses, scattered light, and pointing changes add other signals. Finding oscillations requires modeling this background, checking independent data where possible, and retaining a record of the processing choices.

Modes are spatial patterns

A normal mode is a characteristic spatial oscillation pattern with a characteristic frequency in a linearized model. Radial modes expand and contract spherical layers together. Nonradial modes have regions moving in different phases across the surface and interior. The radial order counts radial structure, spherical degree describes angular complexity, and azimuthal order distinguishes patterns around an axis. Rotation and magnetism can alter this simple classification.

For pressure modes, or p modes, pressure is the main restoring force. For gravity modes, or g modes, buoyancy restores displaced material in stably stratified regions; they are not gravitational waves. Mixed modes have pressure-like behavior in one cavity and gravity-like behavior in another. In evolved stars they can carry information from both the envelope and deep interior.

The Sun's convection stochastically excites many damped modes, giving peaks with finite widths and lifetimes. Classical pulsators can instead have modes driven by heat exchange, including opacity changes that trap and release energy at favorable phases. A driven mode and a stochastically excited mode need different amplitude and lifetime interpretations. Linear theory describes small disturbances; large-amplitude or strongly nonlinear pulsation needs more.

From time series to interior constraints

A frequency spectrum measures how strongly different oscillation frequencies contribute to the data. A peak is not automatically one secure stellar mode: gaps create a spectral window, periodic sampling produces aliases, and a finite observing baseline limits separation of nearby frequencies. Exposure time averages rapid variations. Instrumental trends can masquerade as low-frequency power, while noise can generate peaks that disappear in independent data.

For high-order acoustic modes of a sufficiently slowly rotating star, the large frequency separation is approximately the inverse of the sound travel time across a stellar diameter.

$$\Delta\nu\simeq\left(2\int_0^R\frac{dr}{c_s(r)}\right)^{-1}$$

This connects frequency spacing to internal sound speed. Comparisons to similar models give a mean-density scaling, but structural differences and surface modeling errors require corrections. It is not an exact universal density meter. With temperature, distance, and detailed frequencies, model fitting can constrain radius and mass; inferring age depends particularly on composition, core mixing, and evolutionary assumptions.

Gravity-mode and mixed-mode period spacings probe buoyancy and chemical gradients. They distinguish stars burning hydrogen in a shell from stars also burning helium in their cores, even when surface luminosity and temperature are similar. Rotational splitting separates frequencies that would coincide without rotation and constrains rotation averaged with each mode's sensitivity through the star. A single splitting is not a direct map of rotation at every radius.

Helioseismology can resolve the solar surface and access many angular patterns. Most other stars appear unresolved, so opposite surface regions partly cancel in integrated light and mainly low-degree modes survive. This reduces spatial information but allows comparisons across stellar masses and evolutionary states. Seismic inference tests structure through waves; it does not photograph a stellar core.

WIRE and Buzasi’s collaborative research

WIRE was launched for an infrared survey, but the loss of its cryogenic observing capability prevented that original program. Its star tracker was used for precision stellar photometry. Buzasi and collaborators helped establish this use of the instrument. WIRE's bright-star measurements preceded the much larger oscillation samples of later missions.

Poretti, Buzasi, Laher, Catanzarite, and Conrow studied Theta2 Tauri, identifying several oscillation frequencies and amplitude changes. Bruntt and collaborators, including Buzasi, combined WIRE observations of Epsilon Cephei with ground-based multicolor measurements: a longer ground baseline improved frequency precision, while mode identification remained limited. Retter, Bedding, Buzasi, Kjeldsen, and Kiss examined low-frequency variability in Arcturus and explicitly discussed uncertainty about coherence and lifetime. These are useful examples of what data establish and what they leave unresolved, not interchangeable demonstrations of a complete interior solution.

Later work includes Handler and collaborators' TESS study of the massive pulsator HN Aqr, with Buzasi among the authors, and Metcalfe and collaborators' study of oscillations, rotation, and magnetic activity in 94 Aqr Aa. The Astro2020 white paper led by Huber, also coauthored by Buzasi, sets out research priorities; it is not a new measurement of every prediction it discusses. These studies are included in the stellar research bibliography.

Kepler, TESS, and reproducible inference

Kepler supplied long, precise time series for a large stellar sample in its original field, enabling population studies and detailed mixed-mode measurements. TESS observes broad areas of sky in sectors, emphasizing bright nearby stars; observing duration and cadence vary by target and mission phase. Its long coverage near the ecliptic poles differs from a single-sector light curve. Neither mission observes every mode of every star.

The TESS Asteroseismic Science Operations Center supplies processed data products through TASOC and MAST. A light curve is a research product with a pipeline, version, quality flags, and choices about contamination and detrending. The shortest available cadence is not automatically the best product for every target. One must compare sampling to the oscillation timescale and duration to mode lifetimes and frequency resolution.

Ground-based spectroscopy, networks, and multicolor photometry complement space observations. ESA's PLATO program targets stellar and planetary characterization with asteroseismology among its methods. Mission plans and achieved results must be distinguished. A credible seismic result reports mode selection, noise assumptions, model grid or inversion method, and systematic uncertainty. More detected peaks do not necessarily mean a uniquely determined age or core composition.
