Observing and Classifying Stars

Brightness, distance, and temperature

Apparent brightness describes energy received by a detector, whereas luminosity is energy emitted per unit time. A nearby faint star can appear brighter than a distant luminous one. For isotropic emission without absorption, the same luminosity spreads over larger spheres as distance increases.

$$F=\frac{L}{4\pi d^2}$$

Real observations cover a finite wavelength band and pass through dust and an instrument. Extinction dims and reddens light. Recovering total, or bolometric, luminosity requires a distance, calibration, and corrections for unobserved wavelengths and extinction. Magnitudes are logarithmic measures; they are not luminosities in watts.

Parallax measures the apparent annual angular displacement against distant sources as the observer's position changes. Converting a very uncertain parallax directly into distance can bias the result; survey zero points, selection, and statistical priors matter. A spectrum records intensity versus wavelength. Line identities indicate species, while strengths depend on abundance, excitation, ionization, pressure, and transfer through the atmosphere. A weak line does not automatically mean little of that element.

$$L=4\pi R^2\sigma T_{\rm eff}^4$$

Effective temperature is defined through total emergent flux, even when the spectrum is not an exact blackbody. With luminosity and effective temperature one can infer radius. This inferred radius is not independent evidence if it was computed from the same data used to fit a model. Interferometric angular diameters combined with distances provide a different check for suitable nearby stars.

Reading the Hertzsprung-Russell diagram

The Hertzsprung-Russell diagram places intrinsic luminosity vertically, increasing upward, and effective temperature horizontally, conventionally decreasing toward the right. It is not a map of positions in space. An observational color-magnitude diagram uses related quantities, with color affected by dust and magnitude requiring distance corrections for comparisons of intrinsic brightness.

The main sequence runs from cool, faint, lower-mass hydrogen-burning stars to hot, bright, higher-mass ones. Red giants are cool at the surface yet luminous because they have large radiating areas. White dwarfs can be hot but faint because their radii are small. Supergiants occupy very luminous regions with a range of temperatures. These regions classify observable states; they do not form a single route that every star follows.

An evolutionary track follows one model through time. An isochrone connects models of different initial masses at one age. A cluster's turnoff helps constrain age because the higher-mass members leave core hydrogen burning first. The inference assumes a suitable composition, distance, extinction, and treatment of binaries and rotation; a cluster need not be a perfectly simple population. Crowding and selection can hide faint members.

Weighing stars and testing models

Binary orbits reveal gravity acting on measurable motions. Orbital periods and sizes constrain total mass; velocities of both components constrain the mass ratio. Inclination is essential: a spectroscopic orbit alone often supplies a mass combination containing an unknown inclination. Eclipses, astrometry, and spectroscopy together can greatly reduce this ambiguity. Interacting binaries need additional care because tidal distortion and mass exchange violate simple detached-star assumptions.

Variability can arise from eclipses, spots, pulsation, accretion, or instrument effects. Periodicity alone does not identify the cause. Repeated spectra, different wavelength bands, and time sampling help distinguish them. Rotation broadens lines, but turbulence and instrumental resolution also contribute; the measured projected rotation speed contains an inclination factor.

The strongest tests combine methods with different weaknesses: binary masses, interferometric radii, parallax luminosities, spectral abundances, and seismic frequencies. A model can fit a temperature and luminosity while predicting the wrong internal mixing. Observations of interiors are indirect, but independent constraints make those inferences testable.
