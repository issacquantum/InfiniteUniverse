Quantum Biology

Quantum Biology

Quantum biology studies how quantum processes contribute to biological mechanisms. All molecular biology rests on quantum chemistry, but a more demanding question is whether a particular effect, such as tunneling or spin coherence, changes a reaction rate, energy-transfer pathway, or sensory response in a living system. A molecular effect, its physiological role, and an evolutionary advantage are three different claims.

Molecular structure is the starting point

Electronic states determine the bonds, charge distributions, and interactions of proteins, pigments, and nucleic acids. These properties shape the energy landscape on which molecules move and react. Biological organization adds regulation, compartmentalization, feedback, and evolution. A calculation of one bond does not explain an organism, just as a cellular model does not replace the chemistry of that bond.

Many useful biological models treat nuclear positions, concentrations, or reaction networks classically while obtaining energies and rate parameters from quantum calculations or experiments. This is not a contradiction. The question is whether the approximation retains the observable being studied. If tunneling substantially affects a rate, treating the transferring particle as a classical object crossing a barrier may fail even when the surrounding protein is described approximately classically.

The sequence in this chapter follows the physical task: first an electronic state absorbs light or changes charge; then nuclei and the environment respond; finally chemical products enter a cellular pathway. Vision illustrates a local photochemical change, photosynthesis separates energy transfer from charge transfer, enzymes show how nuclear motion affects rates, and radical pairs add spin-selective chemistry. The later DNA and olfaction sections ask how far those mechanisms can be connected to biological outcomes.

Warm environments, coherence, and timescales

A biomolecule is an open system: it exchanges energy and correlations with solvent, vibrations, and nearby molecules. Decoherence suppresses observable phase relations in a chosen subsystem; relaxation changes energy populations. Neither term means that quantum chemistry stops operating. Different electronic, vibrational, and spin degrees of freedom can have very different lifetimes in the same sample.

Functional relevance depends on competing timescales. A brief coherent motion can matter if a reaction uses it before it decays. Long-lived oscillations can be irrelevant if they do not affect the measured yield. A signal created by carefully timed laboratory laser pulses also need not occur in the same way under ordinary sunlight. Establishing coherence, identifying which states are coherent, and showing a biological benefit require separate evidence.

A useful distinction is between population, the probability of occupying a state, and coherence, the phase relationship between states that can contribute interference terms to a measurement. A transfer process can continue after electronic coherence decays. Nor does an oscillating optical signal uniquely identify electronic coherence: nuclear vibration can modulate the same signal. The interpretation needs the spectrum, the pulse sequence, and a model of how the instrument couples to the states.

Vision: a photon starts a chemical change

In rhodopsin, a protein binds the light-sensitive retinal molecule. Absorption excites the chromophore, meaning the molecular group that absorbs the light, and changes the electronic energy landscape. Retinal can then change from its 11-cis configuration toward an all-trans configuration. Protein rearrangements initiate biochemical signaling that is amplified by the cell. The first photochemical step is quantum; the complete visual response spans molecular, cellular, and neural scales.

Ultrafast spectroscopy and time-resolved structural work probe this early reaction. Schoenlein et al. (1991) (abstract page) measured an approximately 200-femtosecond isomerization, while Ultrafast structural changes direct the first molecular events of vision (2023) resolved early structural changes. Neither result implies that conscious perception remains in a coherent superposition of visual outcomes.

After absorption, the nuclei do not instantly arrive at their final arrangement. They move on an excited-state energy surface. Near a conical intersection, electronic surfaces meet and coupled electronic and nuclear motion allows rapid conversion into a lower-energy electronic state. The protein constrains which geometries are reached and how often the useful product forms. This connects a quantum transition to a chemical yield; it does not mean that every absorbed photon must produce the same product.

Photosynthesis: absorption, transfer, and charge separation

A pigment absorbs a photon and becomes electronically excited. Coupling between pigments allows excitation energy to move toward a reaction center, where charge separation begins the conversion into chemical free energy. Excitation-energy transfer is not the same process as transferring an electron between distant molecules. An exciton describes an electronic excitation that may extend over coupled pigments.

The protein environment shifts energies, provides vibrations, and influences relaxation. Depending on coupling and environmental fluctuations, a useful model may emphasize delocalized excitonic states, transfer between localized states, or an intermediate regime. No general rule says that all environmental disturbance is harmful or that maximal coherence always maximizes useful energy transfer.

Engel et al. (2007) (publication page) reported long-lived beating signals in a photosynthetic complex at low temperature and interpreted them in terms of electronic coherence. Later work distinguished electronic, vibrational, and mixed contributions. Duan et al. (2017) found rapid electronic decoherence under their conditions. These results do not justify saying that plants search all possible paths as a quantum computer.

The established foundation is quantum light absorption and molecular energy transfer. The extent to which particular coherences improve biological performance depends on the complex, conditions, and observable. Cao et al., Quantum biology revisited (2020) reviews why spectroscopic oscillations alone do not settle that functional question. High excitation-transfer yield must also be distinguished from the overall efficiency of converting sunlight into stored biomass.

At weak coupling between pigments, energy transfer can be described as resonant transfer from an excited donor to an acceptor through their electromagnetic interaction, with the donor emission and acceptor absorption spectra providing an overlap condition. No real photon need travel between them. Stronger coupling can require states distributed over several pigments instead. After energy arrives at the reaction center, charge separation must compete successfully with fluorescence, internal conversion, and recombination. A complete account must follow all these channels rather than identify rapid arrival with successful chemical storage.

Worked example: transfer yield is a competition

Consider an illustrative excited state with two independent, irreversible, constant-rate exit channels. Transfer to a reaction center has a characteristic time of 100 picoseconds; all competing losses together have a characteristic time of 1,000 picoseconds. These are chosen teaching values, not measurements of a named organism. A picosecond is one trillionth of a second.

The transfer rate is the reciprocal of its characteristic time: 0.01 per picosecond. The loss rate is 0.001 per picosecond. Add them to obtain a total exit rate of 0.011 per picosecond. Divide the transfer rate by that total: about 0.909, or a 90.9 percent probability of transfer before loss. The mean time before either exit is about 90.9 picoseconds, shorter than either separate waiting time because both channels are available.

If transfer instead takes 200 picoseconds while the loss channel stays unchanged, its rate becomes 0.005 per picosecond. Dividing by the new total of 0.006 gives about 83.3 percent. This quantifies why faster transfer can improve yield. The calculation does not identify coherence as the cause of the speed, include return transfer, or predict overall photosynthetic efficiency. Those require further dynamics and downstream chemistry.

Electron transfer in respiration and photosynthesis

Redox reactions transfer electrons between donors and acceptors. Protein-bound cofactors arrange such transfers into pathways that help convert energy in respiration and photosynthesis. Electron tunneling permits transfer through an intervening barrier without requiring a classical trajectory over it. Its probability depends strongly on electronic coupling and geometry.

Nuclear rearrangement and the surrounding electrostatic environment also matter: the donor, acceptor, protein, and solvent must accommodate the changed charge distribution. Distance alone does not determine the rate. Several short transfers through intermediate cofactors can differ greatly from one long transfer. Their direction and timing must be understood together with free-energy differences and competing reactions.

Moser et al., Nature of biological electron transfer (1992) (publication page) and Page et al., Natural engineering principles of electron tunnelling in biological oxidation-reduction (1999) (publication page) connect molecular organization with electron-transfer behavior. Tunneling in these processes does not imply coherent transport across an entire organism.

The reorganization energy is the energy scale for adjusting nuclear positions and polarization to the changed charge distribution. In the classical nuclear limit of Marcus theory, thermally sampled configurations bring donor and acceptor states into a favorable alignment for transfer, while electronic coupling sets the strength of the transition. This gives a specific reason why increasing the thermodynamic driving force need not always accelerate a reaction. High-frequency quantum vibrations and strongly coupled transfers can require a description beyond that limit. Respiration then connects selected electron transfers to proton pumping and the electrochemical gradient used by ATP synthase; tunneling alone does not supply the free energy of that entire chain.

Hydrogen tunneling and enzyme catalysis

An enzyme changes the energetic and geometric conditions of a reaction. Hydrogen transfer can involve a proton, a hydrogen atom, or a hydride, depending on the chemistry. Because hydrogen nuclei are light, tunneling may contribute appreciably to some transfer steps. This is distinct from the electron tunneling described above; the moving degrees of freedom and their environment are different.

Replacing hydrogen with deuterium changes nuclear mass and vibrational energies. The resulting kinetic isotope effect, a change in reaction rate between isotopic variants, helps probe the mechanism. An isotope effect by itself is not proof of tunneling: zero-point-energy differences and other kinetic steps also matter. Temperature dependence, mutations, structural information, and competing kinetic models strengthen the inference.

Klinman and Kohen, Hydrogen Tunneling Links Protein Dynamics to Enzyme Catalysis (2013) (publication page) examines evidence for hydrogen tunneling and the role of donor-acceptor configurations. A tunneling contribution does not establish that the enzyme evolved specifically to enhance tunneling, or that every catalytic reaction uses it as its dominant mechanism.

The observed rate of an enzyme cycle can be controlled by substrate binding, the chemical transfer step, a conformational change, or product release. A large isotope effect in the isolated chemical step may therefore be masked in the complete cycle. Donor-acceptor separation fluctuates with the protein and solvent; configurations with better overlap can favor transfer. Showing this coupling does not establish that one special protein vibration is uniquely responsible for catalysis. The interpretation must fit both the chemical step and the slower steps measured by the experiment.

Worked example: an isotope effect and a hidden slow step

Suppose an isolated chemical step occurs at 120 per second with hydrogen and 20 per second with deuterium under otherwise matched conditions. The ratio is six. That is a kinetic isotope effect of six, not a measurement that six out of every seven reactions tunneled. A rate ratio compares two complete physical systems whose masses and vibrational energies differ.

Now suppose each chemical event is followed by irreversible product release at 10 per second, with immediate rebinding and no other delays. For this simple sequential cycle, add the mean waiting times. With hydrogen they are one divided by 120 seconds for chemistry and one divided by 10 seconds for release: about 0.1083 seconds in total, or 9.23 cycles per second. With deuterium they total 0.15 seconds, or 6.67 cycles per second. The whole-cycle ratio is only about 1.38.

The slow release step masks much of the chemical isotope effect. Real enzymes can have reversible steps, variable binding, and multiple conformations, so this two-step model is not a universal fitting formula. It shows why measuring turnover alone cannot assign a unique tunneling contribution. Independent measurements of the chemical step, temperature dependence, and structural controls are needed.

Magnetoreception and radical-pair chemistry

A radical pair contains two unpaired electrons whose spin states can be correlated. Interactions with nearby nuclear spins and an external magnetic field influence singlet-triplet evolution. If reaction channels depend on spin, that evolution can alter chemical product yields. A field need not supply the reaction's chemical energy to affect its branching probabilities.

Cryptochrome proteins are candidates for light-dependent magnetic sensing in birds. Xu et al. (2021) (publication page) demonstrated magnetic sensitivity of European robin cryptochrome 4 in vitro. This supports a molecular candidate; it does not by itself identify the complete compass pathway in a living bird.

A biological account must connect molecular orientation, relevant field strengths, reaction lifetimes, signaling, and behavior. Experiments on purified protein, manipulated cells, and navigating animals address different parts of that chain. A result obtained for one species or preparation cannot automatically be generalized to every migratory animal. The proposed radical-pair mechanism remains a specific, testable hypothesis rather than a universal explanation of navigation.

The singlet and triplet labels describe the combined electron-spin state, not two classical arrows with known orientations. Hyperfine interactions couple electron spins to nearby nuclear spins. Their direction dependence can allow the orientation of a molecular radical pair relative to the field to affect reaction yields. For an organism to obtain directional information, orientation averaging must not erase that response, the pair must survive long enough for field-dependent evolution, and a downstream pathway must detect the chemical change amid noise. These requirements explain why a magnetically sensitive molecule is only one part of a compass.

Worked example: a magnetic yield change is not yet a compass

Imagine a controlled radical-pair experiment in which one product forms in 30 percent of reaction events under one field condition and 33 percent under another. The difference is three percentage points. Relative to the original 30 percent yield, it is a 10 percent increase. Confusing those two comparisons would overstate or understate the measured response.

Before attributing the difference to spin dynamics, compare it with statistical uncertainty, drift, illumination changes, heating, and field-independent chemical controls. To argue for directional sensing, vary orientation at the relevant field strength rather than merely compare a large applied field with no field. Then test whether the effect survives realistic molecular disorder and reaches a biological signaling pathway.

These hypothetical numbers illustrate the steps of inference, not a reported cryptochrome result. Even a reproducible yield change establishes neither an animal’s navigation accuracy nor an evolutionary advantage. Molecular chemistry, physiological detection, and behavior must be connected experimentally.

Proton transfer in DNA: a mechanism under investigation

Moving protons along hydrogen bonds can change the tautomeric form of a base, meaning its arrangement of protons and bonds. A rare tautomer may present a different pairing pattern. If such a state persists during a relevant step in replication and escapes correction, it could contribute to a mutation. Formation of a tautomer is therefore not identical to a fixed genetic change.

An open quantum systems approach to proton tunnelling in DNA (2022) models how environmental coupling and tunneling affect proton-transfer populations. It is a theoretical investigation, not a measurement of the fraction of human mutations caused by tunneling. Solvent, sequence, enzymes, strand separation, lifetimes, and repair must be considered before translating a molecular calculation into a mutation rate.

DNA damage, replication errors, chemical modification, and repair already provide many established routes to genetic change. Quantum contributions to particular molecular steps should be evaluated within that biology, not used to replace it with a single explanation.

Olfaction and claims that need stronger evidence

One proposal assigns odor discrimination to electron transfer assisted by molecular vibrations. Isotopic substitution can test aspects of this idea, but it can also alter binding and other chemical properties. Behavioral discrimination and receptor activation are different measurements. Block et al. (2015) found results inconsistent with the vibrational mechanism in the receptors they tested. The proposal should not be presented as an established general theory of smell.

Nor do the mechanisms in this chapter establish quantum theories of consciousness or claims of quantum healing. Such claims need their own defined mechanisms, measurements, and reproducible evidence. Demonstrating molecular coherence in one preparation cannot be used as evidence for unrelated biological or metaphysical conclusions.

Glossary: the mechanisms and the evidence

Chromophore: The molecular group that absorbs light in the transition under discussion.

Exciton: An electronic excitation described in a coupled system; it can be distributed over multiple pigments without being a transferred net charge.

Electronic coupling: An interaction that permits states associated with different sites or configurations to mix or exchange excitation or charge.

Population and coherence: Population describes state occupation probabilities; coherence describes phase relationships that can produce interference. They are not interchangeable measures.

Decoherence and relaxation: Decoherence reduces accessible phase relations in a subsystem; relaxation changes populations toward an energy distribution. Their timescales need not coincide.

Conical intersection: A meeting of molecular electronic energy surfaces that can permit rapid transitions through coupled nuclear and electronic motion.

Reorganization energy: The energy scale of nuclear and environmental adjustment associated with a change in charge distribution.

Quantum tunneling: Transmission or transfer through a barrier inaccessible to the corresponding classical trajectory at the same energy.

Kinetic isotope effect: A ratio of reaction rates after isotopic substitution under specified conditions; it is evidence about a mechanism, not a unique test for tunneling.

Radical pair: Two radicals with unpaired electron spins whose combined state can influence spin-selective chemistry.

Singlet and triplet: Combined two-electron spin sectors with total spin zero and one, respectively.

Hyperfine interaction: Coupling between electronic and nuclear magnetic moments that can affect spin evolution.

Quantum yield: The number of specified photochemical events per absorbed photon under stated conditions; it is not automatically an energy-conversion efficiency.

Tautomer: A molecular form differing in proton positions and bonding pattern, not a different DNA sequence by itself.

Functional evidence: Evidence that changing a mechanism changes a biological outcome; evidence of adaptive advantage requires an additional evolutionary argument.

How a biological quantum claim is tested

Start with a measurable outcome: reaction rate, product yield, energy-transfer time, or controlled sensory response. Specify the preparation and conditions. Compare predictions with a model that lacks the proposed effect, without giving the competing model unrealistically poor parameters. Isotope substitution, magnetic fields, temperature changes, and targeted molecular changes can help isolate mechanisms, but each intervention also needs controls for ordinary chemical or structural changes.

The strongest argument connects a measured microscopic effect to a change in biological function and survives independent tests. Evidence for evolutionary selection requires a further comparison of biological performance and alternatives. This distinction allows quantum biology to be both ambitious and precise: well-established chemistry, active mechanistic research, and speculative extensions remain identifiable.
