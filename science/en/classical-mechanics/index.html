Classical Mechanics

Meaning and Scope

Classical mechanics describes how bodies move and how interactions change that motion. It connects measured positions and times with forces, energy, and momentum. It remains useful for vehicles, structures, instruments, robots, and planetary motion when quantum and relativistic corrections are negligible.

The word mechanics has roots associated with machines and their operation. Its historical meaning is broader than the modern equations of motion.

Source of etymological origin: Online Etymology Dictionary: mechanics.

From Falling Bodies to General Laws

Describing a trajectory is different from explaining why it occurs. Galileo's Two New Sciences (1638) developed mathematical accounts of accelerated motion and projectiles. Inclined planes made changes in speed easier to measure than a rapid vertical fall. His account of uniformly accelerated motion relates distance from rest to the square of elapsed time. The Third Day connects the mathematical argument with an experimental procedure.

Newton's Principia, first published in 1687, brought laws of motion and universal gravitation into one framework for terrestrial and celestial motion. The 1687 front matter preserved by the Newton Project records that program. Later analytical formulations made constrained and many-body problems easier to organize. They did not discard the successful Newtonian predictions.

Kepler connected planetary orbits with ellipses and swept areas in Astronomia nova (1609); the period-distance relation appeared in Harmonices mundi (1619). Euler organized free and constrained motion through analysis in Mechanica (1736). Lagrange developed a general treatment using coordinates and variation in Méchanique analitique (1788). Poincaré’s three-body work (1890) contributed to qualitative analysis of trajectories and stability, preceding modern nonlinear dynamics. These contributions answer distinct problems: describing orbits, organizing equations, and understanding their behavior without always having explicit solutions.

Measurement, Vectors, and Initial Conditions

A prediction needs a defined system, a reference frame, units, and initial data. Treating a vehicle as a particle keeps its mass and position but ignores its dimensions and internal motion. A rigid-body model retains its orientation and rotation while neglecting deformation. Neither is suitable for every question.

In the International System of Units (SI), position uses metres (m), time seconds (s), and mass kilograms (kg). A scalar, such as mass or speed, has a magnitude without a spatial direction. A vector, such as displacement or force, has magnitude and direction. Components give its signed projections onto chosen axes. A displacement of 3 m east and 4 m north has magnitude 5 m, not 7 m. Distance traveled, unlike displacement, depends on the path.

A derivative is an instantaneous rate of change; an integral accumulates contributions over an interval or path. A dot over a quantity means a time derivative. Bold symbols denote vectors. Dimensions provide a useful check: force has units kg m/s², called newtons (N), so force divided by mass has acceleration units. Dimensional agreement is necessary, but cannot establish the correct sign, coefficient, or physical model.

Measurement uncertainty and model assumptions belong in the result. To predict an ordinary second-order equation of motion, specify initial position and velocity. More precise initial data cannot repair an incorrect force law.

The dot product \(\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B=AB\cos\theta\) multiplies magnitudes and the cosine of their angle, producing a scalar. Thus a 10 N force at 60° to a 2 m displacement does 10 J of work. The cross product has magnitude \(AB\sin\theta\) and points perpendicular to both vectors, following the right-hand rule from the first vector toward the second. Reversing their order reverses its direction. These operations distinguish work from torque.

Position, Velocity, and Acceleration

Kinematics describes motion without yet specifying its cause. Velocity measures change of position; acceleration measures change of velocity, including changes of direction. A body can accelerate while its speed stays constant, as in uniform circular motion. The last expression below applies to one coordinate with constant acceleration and initial time zero.

For an object starting at \(x_0=0\) with \(v_0=3\) m/s and constant \(a=2\) m/s², after 4 s its position is \(3(4)+\tfrac12(2)(4^2)=28\) m and its velocity is 11 m/s. If acceleration varies, integrate it rather than inserting an average into a formula without justification.

A projectile near Earth's surface separates into horizontal constant-velocity motion and vertical acceleration approximately equal to \(-g\), with upward positive. This yields a parabola only when air resistance, Earth's curvature, and variation of \(g\) can be neglected. Measurements of position against time test the prediction, rather than the appearance of the curve alone.

$$ \mathbf v=\frac{d\mathbf r}{dt},\quad\mathbf a=\frac{d\mathbf v}{dt},\qquad x=x_0+v_0t+\tfrac12at^2 $$

Newton's Three Laws

First law: in an inertial frame, a body with zero net external force has constant velocity. Rest is one case, not the only case. Sustained motion does not require a sustained net force; friction often hides this in daily experience.

Second law: net force changes momentum. For constant mass it becomes the equation below. The force is the vector sum of all external forces on the chosen body. It determines acceleration, not the direction of velocity. A 2 kg cart pulled by 10 N against 2 N of resistance accelerates at \((10-2)/2=4\) m/s².

Third law: in the Newtonian interaction model, the force of body A on B is opposite to that of B on A. These forces act on different bodies and do not cancel in one body's force diagram. For an isolated collection with such internal pairs, they cancel in the total momentum balance. Electromagnetic interactions require including field momentum when this simple particle-only balance is inadequate.

Start a force diagram by isolating one body, naming the external interactions, and choosing axes. Only then resolve components and solve. The Feynman lectures on dynamics develop the relation between forces, acceleration, and changing vector direction.

$$ \mathbf{F}=m\mathbf{a} $$

Common Forces and Their Approximations

Weight near Earth's surface is approximately \(mg\), with \(g\approx9.81\) m/s². Mass measures inertia; weight is a force. A surface's normal force acts perpendicular to it and adjusts to contact conditions. It is not automatically equal to weight. An ideal taut, massless string transmits tension along its length; an ideal spring has a restoring force proportional to displacement.

Dry static friction opposes impending relative slip and adjusts up to a limiting magnitude. Sliding friction is often approximated by a constant coefficient times normal force. If \(N=20\) N and \(\mu_s=0.4\), the static limit is 8 N. A 3 N horizontal pull can be balanced by 3 N of friction, not necessarily 8 N. The coefficients depend on surfaces and conditions; these laws are approximations, not microscopic explanations.

Drag depends on motion relative to a surrounding medium. A linear drag approximation is useful in some slow viscous regimes; a quadratic approximation is useful in other flow regimes. Neither applies at every speed. Contact, deformation, and flow can require more detailed material models.

$$ \mathbf W=m\mathbf g,\quad F_x=-kx,\qquad |f_s|\leq\mu_s N,\quad |f_k|=\mu_k N $$

Work and Mechanical Energy

A force transfers energy by doing work along a displacement. Only its component along that displacement contributes, through the dot product. The net work on a particle equals its change of kinetic energy. Work and energy use joules (J), where 1 J = 1 N m. Power is the rate of energy transfer, in watts (J/s).

A constant net force of 6 N along a 5 m displacement does 30 J of work. A 2 kg body starting from rest therefore reaches \(\sqrt{30}\approx5.48\) m/s. A force perpendicular to velocity does no instantaneous work; it can change direction without changing speed.

A conservative force admits a potential energy \(U\), so its work is minus the change in \(U\). For a one-dimensional force, \(F_x=-dU/dx\). With no other work and no explicit time dependence, \(K+U\) remains constant. Friction can reduce this mechanical energy while increasing internal energy. That is not a failure of total energy conservation. Potential energy has an arbitrary additive zero; differences affect motion. See the discussion of work and potential energy.

$$ W_{\rm net}=\int_A^B\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r=\Delta K,\qquad K=\tfrac12mv^2 $$

Instantaneous power is \(P=\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf v_{\mathrm{app}}\), where \(\mathbf v_{\mathrm{app}}\) is the velocity of the force's application point in the chosen frame. For a particle, that is its velocity; for a rotating body it need not be the center-of-mass velocity. A 20 N pull along an application-point velocity of 3 m/s supplies 60 W. For a time-independent conservative potential, \(W_{A\to B}=U(A)-U(B)\) and \(\mathbf F=-\nabla U\): the gradient collects spatial derivatives of potential energy. If the potential explicitly changes with time, \(d(K+U)/dt=\partial U/\partial t\) when no other forces do work. Conservative spatial dependence alone therefore does not guarantee energy conservation in a driven system.

Momentum, Impulse, and Collisions

Momentum is mass times velocity in the Newtonian regime. Impulse accumulates force over time and equals the change of momentum. For a system, total momentum is conserved when the net external impulse vanishes, even if large internal forces act during a collision. If the net external force remains zero, the center of mass moves at constant velocity.

A 1 kg cart moving at 4 m/s sticks to a stationary 3 kg cart on a horizontal track. Neglecting external impulse, total momentum is 4 kg m/s and combined mass is 4 kg, so the common final velocity is 1 m/s. Kinetic energy falls from 8 J to 2 J; the remaining 6 J becomes deformation, heat, and other internal motion.

An elastic collision conserves total kinetic energy as well as momentum in the isolated system. An inelastic collision does not conserve kinetic energy; sticking is the perfectly inelastic case. In a two-dimensional collision, conserve momentum component by component. Momentum alone does not determine every outgoing velocity without additional collision information. Conservation of momentum explains why choosing the system boundary matters.

$$ \mathbf p=m\mathbf v,\qquad\Delta\mathbf p=\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\mathbf F_{\rm ext}\,dt $$

For a chosen collection of particles with constant masses, \(\mathbf R_{\mathrm{CM}}=\sum_i m_i\mathbf r_i/M_{\mathrm{tot}}\), with \(M_{\mathrm{tot}}=\sum_i m_i\). The index \(i\) labels each particle; all positions use the same origin and are measured in metres. A 1 kg mass at \(x=0\) and a 3 kg mass at \(x=4\) m have \(X_{\mathrm{CM}}=(1\cdot0+3\cdot4)/4=3\) m. The center lies nearer the heavier mass. Differentiating twice gives \(M_{\mathrm{tot}}\ddot{\mathbf R}_{\mathrm{CM}}=\mathbf F_{\mathrm{ext}}\) when internal forces cancel. A rocket or another system exchanging matter across its boundary needs the momentum carried by that matter; the constant-mass formula is not its complete balance.

$$\mathbf R_{\mathrm{CM}}=\frac{\sum_i m_i\mathbf r_i}{M_{\mathrm{tot}}},\quad M_{\mathrm{tot}}=\sum_i m_i$$

Rotation and Equilibrium

A force can change translation and rotation. Its torque about a chosen origin is the cross product of lever arm and force. Its magnitude is force times perpendicular lever arm; its direction follows the right-hand rule. Angular momentum measures rotational motion about that same origin. For a fixed inertial origin, external torque gives its rate of change.

The moment of inertia \(I\) measures how mass is distributed relative to an axis, in kg m². It is not just total mass. For a rigid body about a fixed axis, net axial torque is \(I\alpha\), where \(\alpha\) is angular acceleration. A perpendicular 20 N force at 0.30 m produces 6 N m; with \(I=2\) kg m², the angular acceleration is 3 rad/s². General three-dimensional rotation needs an inertia tensor, not one scalar for every direction.

Static equilibrium requires both zero net force and zero net torque, with the body initially at rest. Two opposite forces can have zero resultant but still produce a turning couple. Rolling without slipping imposes \(v_{\mathrm{CM}}=R\omega\) on level ground, where CM denotes center of mass; its kinetic energy includes both translation and rotation. Rotation in space develops the vector description.

$$ \boldsymbol\tau=\mathbf r\times\mathbf F,\quad\frac{d\mathbf L}{dt}=\boldsymbol\tau_{\rm ext},\qquad\tau_z=I_z\alpha $$

For a particle, \(\mathbf L=\mathbf r\times\mathbf p\), in kg m²/s. The position vector starts at the chosen origin, so changing that origin can change angular momentum. A particle moving directly toward the origin has zero angular momentum about it even with nonzero momentum. For a rigid body, \(I=\sum_i m_i r_{\perp i}^{2}\), where \(r_{\perp i}\) is each mass's perpendicular distance from the axis. Moving two 1 kg masses from 0.5 m to 1 m from the same axis increases \(I\) from 0.5 to 2 kg m² without changing total mass. Fixed-axis rotational energy is \(K_{\mathrm{rot}}=\tfrac12I\omega^2\). In three dimensions, the inertia tensor is a matrix connecting angular-velocity components to angular-momentum components; it accounts for axis dependence and coupling between directions, so the two vectors need not be parallel.

Consider a uniform solid cylinder of mass \(m\), radius \(R\), and \(I_{\mathrm{CM}}=\tfrac12mR^2\), released from rest down a fixed incline through height \(h=1\) m. Assume sufficient static friction for no slip, no deformation, no rolling resistance, and negligible air drag. The contact point is instantaneously at rest, so ideal static friction does no work, although it supplies torque. Energy gives \(mgh=\tfrac12mv_{\mathrm{CM}}^2+\tfrac12I_{\mathrm{CM}}\omega^2=\tfrac34mv_{\mathrm{CM}}^2\), using \(v_{\mathrm{CM}}=R\omega\). Thus \(v_{\mathrm{CM}}=\sqrt{4gh/3}\approx3.62\) m/s. The friction is generally nonzero here; slipping or a moving contact surface changes the work and constraints.

$$\mathbf L=\mathbf r\times\mathbf p,\quad I=\sum_i m_i r_{\perp i}^2,\quad K_{\mathrm{rot}}=\tfrac12I\omega^2$$

Gravitation and Orbits

Newtonian gravity attracts two point masses with a force proportional to their masses and inversely proportional to separation squared. Outside a spherical mass distribution, its gravitational field is that of its mass concentrated at the center. Here \(\mathbf r\) points from the central mass \(M\) to the body \(m\); the negative sign makes the force attractive. \(G\) is the gravitational constant.

A satellite continuously falls while its sideways velocity keeps it from reaching the surface. Equating \(mv^2/r\) with \(GMm/r^2\) gives the circular speed below when \(m\) is negligible compared with \(M\). With Earth's \(GM\approx3.986\times10^{14}\) m³/s² and radius from the center \(r=7.0\times10^6\) m, speed is about 7.55 km/s and period about 97 minutes. This ignores atmosphere, nonspherical gravity, other bodies, and relativistic corrections.

The inverse-square two-body problem yields conic trajectories: bound orbits are ellipses, with the circle as a special case. Conservation of angular momentum gives equal swept areas in equal times; the orbital period squared scales with semimajor axis cubed for fixed total mass. These are the Kepler relations recovered by Newtonian dynamics. The gravitation lecture relates these predictions to astronomical observations. Other planets perturb the ideal two-body orbit. For comparable masses, both bodies move about their common center of mass, and the relative-motion gravitational parameter uses their sum.

$$ \mathbf F=-\frac{GMm}{r^3}\mathbf r,\quad U=-\frac{GMm}{r},\qquad v_c=\sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}} $$

Kepler's first law: a bound, noncollision relative orbit is an ellipse with the force center at a focus, not generally at the ellipse's geometric center. The semimajor axis \(a\) is half its longest diameter; eccentricity \(e\) measures elongation, with \(0\le e<1\) and \(e=0\) a circle. The focus lies \(ae\) from the center, giving nearest and farthest separations \(a(1-e)\) and \(a(1+e)\). Second law: the line joining the bodies sweeps equal areas in equal times, so they move faster near closest approach. Third law: \(T_{\mathrm{orb}}^2=4\pi^2a^3/[G(M+m)]\). Here \(T_{\mathrm{orb}}\) is the period in seconds, not kinetic energy, and \(a\) is the semimajor axis of the relative orbit, not either body's individual orbit about the common center of mass.

For an isolated pair, introduce reduced mass \(\mu=Mm/(M+m)\) and relative speed \(v_{\mathrm{rel}}\). Energy after subtracting center-of-mass translation is \(E_{\mathrm{rel}}=\tfrac12\mu v_{\mathrm{rel}}^2-GMm/r\), with zero potential at infinite separation. For noncollision Kepler trajectories, negative energy gives an ellipse, zero a parabola, and positive a hyperbola. At the escape threshold, final separation tends to infinity and relative speed tends to zero, so \(\tfrac12\mu v_{\mathrm{esc}}^2=GMm/r\), yielding \(v_{\mathrm{esc}}=\sqrt{2G(M+m)/r}\). For \(m\ll M\), this becomes \(\sqrt{2GM/r}\). There is no atmosphere, propulsion, or third-body interaction in this derivation. Comparable masses both accelerate: their distances from the center of mass are \(mr/(M+m)\) and \(Mr/(M+m)\).

As an elliptical example with negligible satellite mass, take Earth's \(GM=3.986\times10^{14}\) m³/s², \(a=1.0\times10^7\) m and \(e=0.30\). The nearest separation is \(7.0\times10^6\) m, the farthest \(1.3\times10^7\) m, and the period is approximately 9952 s, or 166 minutes. Taking Earth's radius as \(6.37\times10^6\) m, the nearest altitude is approximately 630 km, not 7000 km. Escape speed at that closest separation is about 10.67 km/s. Altitude is center-to-center distance minus the central body's radius.

$$\begin{aligned}T_{\mathrm{orb}}^2&=\frac{4\pi^2a^3}{G(M+m)},\\E_{\mathrm{rel}}&=\tfrac12\mu v_{\mathrm{rel}}^2-\frac{GMm}{r},\\\mu&=\frac{Mm}{M+m},\quad v_{\mathrm{esc}}=\sqrt{\frac{2G(M+m)}{r}}.\end{aligned}$$

Circular Motion and the Vector Model

Uniform circular motion has constant speed, but changing velocity. For radius \(R\) and angular speed \(\omega\), the acceleration points toward the center and has magnitude \(\omega^2R=v^2/R\). Centripetal names this inward net-force requirement, not an additional interaction: tension, friction, gravity, or another physical force must supply it.

Oscillations, Stability, and Chaos

An equation is linear when the unknown variable and its derivatives occur to first power with coefficients independent of that variable. A force proportional to displacement therefore permits superposition of solutions of the free equation. A sine of the angle or a cube of displacement makes an equation nonlinear; that alone does not establish chaos.

An equilibrium is a configuration that remains at rest under the stated forces. Near a smooth minimum of potential energy, a small displacement often produces an approximately linear restoring force. The resulting harmonic oscillator appears in springs, suspensions, and normal modes of vibrating structures. A pendulum is approximately harmonic only at small angles. The harmonic-oscillator lecture develops this approximation.

In the equation below, \(m\) is mass, \(b\) viscous damping, \(k\) stiffness, and \(F_0\cos(\Omega t)\) a periodic drive. With no damping or drive, angular frequency is \(\omega_0=\sqrt{k/m}\). For \(m=0.50\) kg and \(k=200\) N/m, \(\omega_0=20\) rad/s and the period is approximately 0.314 s. Damping removes mechanical energy; a drive supplies it. Resonance is a large response over an appropriate frequency range, not unlimited growth in every real system.

Stability asks whether small disturbances remain small. A local potential minimum is a useful stability test for conservative equilibria, but driven and dissipative systems need a more general analysis. Chaos is deterministic dynamics with sensitive dependence on initial conditions in the relevant region of state space. A double pendulum can exhibit it, but not every oscillation or nonlinear trajectory is chaotic. Exponential separation of nearby states can limit prediction even when the force law is known. It does not license arbitrary motion or violate conservation laws.

$$ m\ddot x+b\dot x+kx=F_0\cos(\Omega t),\qquad\omega_0=\sqrt{k/m} $$

The potential-minimum argument requires more than smoothness: at equilibrium \(U'(x_0)=0\), and a nondegenerate stable minimum has \(U''(x_0)>0\). For displacement \(y=x-x_0\), Taylor expansion gives \(U(x)\approx U(x_0)+\tfrac12U''(x_0)y^2\). Primes mean derivatives with respect to position. The resulting force is approximately \(-k_{\mathrm{eff}}y\), with effective stiffness \(k_{\mathrm{eff}}=U''(x_0)\) in N/m and \(\omega_0=\sqrt{k_{\mathrm{eff}}/m}\). Higher terms must remain small over the motion. For \(U=\lambda x^4\), \(\lambda>0\) in J/m⁴, the minimum is stable but degenerate: its second derivative vanishes at zero and the leading force is \(-4\lambda x^3\). There is no nonzero amplitude-independent harmonic frequency from the quadratic approximation.

For an ideal pendulum, take a point mass \(m\), a massless rigid rod of length \(\ell\), a fixed frictionless pivot, and uniform gravity. With angle \(\theta\) from downward vertical, gravitational torque is \(-mg\ell\sin\theta\) and inertia about the pivot is \(m\ell^2\). Thus \(m\ell^2\ddot\theta=-mg\ell\sin\theta\). At \(|\theta|\ll1\), measured in radians, \(\sin\theta\approx\theta\) gives \(\ddot\theta+(g/\ell)\theta=0\) and \(\omega_0=\sqrt{g/\ell}\). Large-angle motion is nonlinear and amplitude-dependent; the ideal unforced single pendulum is not thereby chaotic.

For \(m,k>0\), define \(\gamma=b/(2m)\) in s⁻¹. Without a drive, \(b=0\) gives free undamped motion. With \(0<\gamma<\omega_0\), motion is underdamped: it oscillates at \(\sqrt{\omega_0^2-\gamma^2}\) under a decaying exponential envelope. At \(\gamma=\omega_0\), critical damping separates oscillatory and nonoscillatory decay; at \(\gamma>\omega_0\), overdamping gives two decaying exponential contributions, with a slower long-time return. These classifications concern the homogeneous equation, not the imposed drive.

For positive damping, the initial-condition-dependent transient decays, leaving a periodic steady response to a sinusoidal drive. Its displacement amplitude is \(A=F_0/\sqrt{(k-m\Omega^2)^2+(b\Omega)^2}\), with phase lag \(\delta\) determined by the quadrant-correct relation \(\delta=\operatorname{atan2}(b\Omega,k-m\Omega^2)\). Phase indicates position within an oscillation cycle; a phase difference measures how far one oscillation leads another. The displacement peak is at \(\Omega_{\mathrm{peak}}=\sqrt{\omega_0^2-2\gamma^2}\) only if \(\gamma<\omega_0/\sqrt2\). Otherwise the maximum occurs at zero frequency; a nonzero-frequency displacement resonance is absent.

For the 0.50 kg, 200 N/m spring above, choose \(b=2\) kg/s and \(F_0=1\) N. Then \(\gamma=2\) s⁻¹, the transient envelope decays by a factor \(e\) in 0.5 s, and the displacement peak is near 19.80 rad/s. At a 20 rad/s drive the steady amplitude is 0.025 m and lag is \(\pi/2\) radians. Two equal masses coupled by equal springs can move together or oppositely: these fixed relative patterns are normal modes of the linearized system. A real string has distributed mass and many vibration modes; one mass and spring represents a selected mode, not the complete instrument.

$$\begin{aligned}U(x)&\approx U(x_0)+\tfrac12U^{\prime\prime}(x_0)(x-x_0)^2,\\k_{\mathrm{eff}}&=U^{\prime\prime}(x_0)>0,\quad\omega_0=\sqrt{k_{\mathrm{eff}}/m}.\end{aligned}$$
$$m\ell^2\ddot\theta=-mg\ell\sin\theta,\quad\ddot\theta+\frac{g}{\ell}\theta\approx0$$

A concrete example is releasing a double pendulum twice from nearly identical positions and nearly zero initial velocities. In a chaotic regime, the trajectories initially track each other, but later the arms can point in very different directions. Shinbrot, Grebogi, Wisdom and Yorke (1992) studied this sensitivity experimentally and numerically. Not every initial condition is chaotic. Deterministic equations assign an evolution to exact initial data; measurement uncertainty prevents knowing those data exactly and limits the trajectory prediction horizon. That differs from introducing random forces. An ideal double pendulum can conserve energy and remain spatially bounded while nearby trajectories diverge; energy conservation restricts motion but does not fix its future phase.

Frames, Constraints, and Coordinates

Inertial frames moving at constant relative velocity share Newton's laws under Galilean transformations: velocities differ by the frame velocity, while accelerations agree. An accelerating or rotating frame needs inertial-force terms. The centrifugal term in a rotating frame is not an outward partner force acting on the same body in an inertial frame; the Coriolis term depends on velocity relative to the rotating frame.

A constraint restricts allowed motion. A bead on a wire or a pendulum of fixed length has fewer independent coordinates than an unconstrained particle. A pendulum can be described by one angle rather than two independent Cartesian coordinates. These independent variables are generalized coordinates, and their number is the number of degrees of freedom.

Constraints expressible as relations among coordinates and time are called holonomic. Rolling constraints can instead involve velocities and may be nonholonomic; they cannot always be removed by simply choosing fewer coordinates. Ideal constraint forces do no work on allowed virtual displacements, which helps derive the standard Lagrange equations. Frictional or externally driven constraints require additional care.

A train moves east at 10 m/s. A ball moving east at 12 m/s relative to the ground has velocity 2 m/s relative to the train: \(x'=x-Vt\), \(v'=v-V\), and \(a'=a\) for constant frame velocity \(V\). If the train accelerates at 1 m/s², a freely sliding 2 kg object appears to accelerate backward; the train-frame inertial force is -2 N. It is a coordinate correction, not an additional interaction with a partner body. On a rotating platform, an object at rest relative to the platform needs an outward centrifugal term in that frame; a sliding object also has a Coriolis term perpendicular to its relative velocity and the rotation axis. Neither is the Newton-third-law partner of the inward interaction force.

A pendulum's fixed-length relation \(x^2+y^2=\ell^2\) is holonomic: it restricts positions directly. A wheel rolling on a plane without sideways slip constrains its velocity in the direction perpendicular to its heading; the restriction generally cannot be integrated into a relation among positions alone and is nonholonomic. Rolling along a fixed straight line is a special integrable case. Configuration space lists independent positions or angles. Phase space adds their conjugate momenta: one mass on a line has a one-dimensional configuration coordinate \(x\), but a two-dimensional state \((x,p)\).

Lagrangian and Hamiltonian Views

Instead of separately tracking every constraint force, analytical mechanics works with coordinates and an action. For many conservative mechanical systems, the Lagrangian is kinetic minus potential energy, \(L=T-U\); here \(T\) means the same kinetic energy called \(K\) earlier. The action integrates \(L\) over a path and has units J s. Stationarity means that the first-order change of action vanishes for small allowed variations with fixed endpoints. It is not necessarily a minimum, and it does not mean a body deliberately compares future paths.

The first two cards give the action condition and its Euler-Lagrange equation for each independent coordinate. For \(L=\tfrac12m\dot x^2-U(x)\), differentiating with respect to \(\dot x\) gives \(m\dot x\), and differentiating with respect to \(x\) gives \(-dU/dx\). The equation therefore returns \(m\ddot x=-dU/dx\): Newton's law for that conservative force. Nonconservative generalized forces must be added when present. Sussman and Wisdom's first chapter explains the variational construction and constraints.

Define canonical momentum by \(p_i=\partial L/\partial\dot q_i\). When this relation can be inverted for velocities, the Legendre transform gives \(H=\sum_i p_i\dot q_i-L\). The Hamilton equations below evolve coordinates and momenta in phase space. Subscript \(i\) labels independent coordinates; the card writes one pair. Canonical momentum need not equal mass times velocity, and \(H\) is not automatically the ordinary total energy in every coordinate choice. An explicitly time-independent Hamiltonian is conserved along its solutions.

Hamilton's 1834 paper belongs to the historical development of this analytical approach. Canonical transformations change phase-space coordinates while retaining Hamilton's form; they connect mechanics with perturbation theory, statistical mechanics, and quantum theory without making the classical model itself quantum.

$$ S[q]=\int_{t_1}^{t_2}L(q,\dot q,t)\,dt,\qquad \delta S=0 $$
$$ \frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q}-\frac{\partial L}{\partial q}=0 $$
$$ \dot q=\frac{\partial H}{\partial p},\qquad\dot p=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial q} $$

For the same unforced spring, Newton gives \(m\ddot x=-kx\). With \(L=\tfrac12m\dot x^2-\tfrac12kx^2\), the two derivatives in the Euler-Lagrange equation are \(d(m\dot x)/dt=m\ddot x\) and \(-kx\), returning \(m\ddot x+kx=0\). Momentum is \(p=m\dot x\). The Legendre transform exchanges the velocity variable for its momentum: substituting \(\dot x=p/m\) into \(p\dot x-L\) gives \(H=p^2/(2m)+kx^2/2\). Hamilton's equations give \(\dot x=p/m\) and \(\dot p=-kx\); differentiating the first reproduces the same Newton equation. This Hamiltonian is separable, meaning a sum of a momentum-only kinetic term and a position-only potential term.

A canonical transformation changes paired coordinates and momenta while preserving Hamilton's equations and their phase-space geometry. For a constant nonzero scale \(c\), \(Q=cx\), \(P=p/c\) is a simple example: coordinate expansion is compensated by momentum contraction. Arbitrarily changing both variables does not have this property. Hamilton's 1834 paper developed the characteristic-function approach; his Second Essay (1835) developed the principal function and the Hamiltonian equations of motion.

Energy, Momentum, and Symmetry

A symmetry is a transformation that leaves the relevant laws or action unchanged. Noether's 1918 first theorem associates continuous symmetries of an action with conserved quantities along solutions, under its variational assumptions. This explains a relationship among conservation laws, rather than assuming every system conserves every quantity.

For ordinary isolated mechanical systems, invariance under spatial translations yields momentum conservation, rotations yield angular momentum conservation, and time translations yield energy conservation. An external potential fixed in space can break translation symmetry for the selected subsystem. A time-dependent drive can exchange energy with it. To decide what is conserved, identify the system and its actual symmetry. The English translation linked here is from 1971; its 2005 repository upload is not the theorem's publication date.

For a free particle on a line, \(L=m\dot x^2/2\) is unchanged under \(x\mapsto x+\epsilon\), where \(\epsilon\) is a constant displacement. Since \(\partial L/\partial x=0\), Euler-Lagrange gives \(d(m\dot x)/dt=0\): translation invariance conserves momentum. Adding a spring fixed to the laboratory gives \(L=m\dot x^2/2-kx^2/2\); translation now changes the potential and \(\dot p=-kx\). Momentum of the mass alone is not conserved, although its time-independent mechanical energy is. If the spring support is driven, the mass can exchange energy with that external drive. Including the support and driver changes the system boundary and the relevant balance.

Many-Body and Continuum Mechanics

A gas, bridge, instrument, or galaxy can require many coupled degrees of freedom. A rigid-body approximation omits deformation; elasticity describes it; continuum mechanics replaces individual particles with fields such as mass density and velocity. A continuum description is useful only when the scales of interest justify averaging over the underlying structure.

Balance laws still govern mass, momentum, and energy, but material relations are also needed: conservation alone does not specify a fluid's viscosity or a solid's elastic response. A few vibration modes can approximate an instrument, while molecular dynamics follows individual particles. Statistical mechanics treats collective behavior without predicting every microscopic trajectory.

Numerical Methods and Error Checks

A differential equation gives a rate of change, not a ready-made trajectory. An integrator approximates that trajectory with finite time steps. Explicit Euler uses the current slope; its accumulated error is generally first order. For an undamped oscillator it can generate spurious energy, even when the animation looks smooth. Higher order alone does not guarantee that long-term physical behavior is reliable.

The velocity Verlet update below uses endpoint accelerations for a constant-mass, position-dependent force. For smooth solutions its global error is second order: halving the step should reduce it by roughly four over a fixed interval. With a separable conservative Hamiltonian and fixed step it is symplectic, preserving the appropriate phase-space geometry, not exact energy at every step. On a harmonic oscillator the stability condition is \(h\omega_0<2\); useful accuracy normally requires a much smaller step.

Compare an oscillator with its analytic period and phase. For an orbit, monitor energy and angular momentum, repeat with smaller steps, and check close approaches. Constraint error, roundoff, and force-model error are different from time-discretization error. Adaptive steps and velocity-dependent drag need methods designed for them. A small energy error does not prove a trajectory is accurate, especially in a chaotic system.

Verlet's 1967 molecular-dynamics paper is a historical source for the integration family, not a claim that the exact velocity-update notation here appeared there. Marsden and West's 2001 review develops discrete variational mechanics and the resulting geometric integrators.

$$ \begin{aligned}x_{n+1}&=x_n+v_n h+\tfrac12a_nh^2,\\a_{n+1}&=a(x_{n+1}),\\v_{n+1}&=v_n+\tfrac12(a_n+a_{n+1})h.\end{aligned} $$

A reproducible refinement check uses \(m=1\) kg, \(k=1\) N/m, \(x(0)=1\) m and \(v(0)=0\). The exact result is \(x(t)=\cos(t)\) m with time in seconds. At \(t=1\) s, velocity Verlet gives approximately 0.538893 m for \(h=0.2\) s and 0.539951 m for \(h=0.1\) s, compared with 0.540302 m exactly. The position error falls from 0.001409 to 0.000351 m, close to a factor of four. Its numerical frequency is \(2\arcsin(h\omega_0/2)/h\); at \(h=0.2\) s it is about 1.001674 rad/s. After 1000 s the accumulated phase lead is about 1.674 radians, even though the relative energy error stays within 1% in exact arithmetic for this example. Phase error is a timing error within the oscillation, not necessarily a large amplitude error.

Fixed-step geometric methods target the structure of conservative motion. Adaptive high-order methods instead adjust the step to resolve rapid changes and meet error tolerances. Rein and Spiegel's IAS15 (Implicit integrator with Adaptive timeStepping, 15th order; 2015 journal issue, first published online in 2014) uses Gauss-Radau quadrature for gravitational dynamics, including close encounters and nonconservative forces. It is not a formally symplectic map. Its reported long-integration accuracy concerns tested problems and implementations, not a guarantee for every trajectory. Unplanned variable steps can destroy a fixed-step method's symplectic property; tolerances, roundoff, cost, and the physical question determine the appropriate choice.

Modern Methods and Research Questions

Spacecraft navigation, robotics, structural analysis, and biomechanics combine mechanical equations with measurements and uncertain parameters. A useful model must be tested against observations in its intended regime. Matching one trajectory does not identify a unique force law, and calibration data do not guarantee reliability under different loads or initial conditions.

Hamiltonian Neural Networks (Greydanus, Dzamba, and Yosinski, 2019) learn a scalar Hamiltonian and obtain a vector field through Hamilton's equations. This builds a mechanical structure into learning rather than fitting arbitrary time derivatives. The paper tests the idea on controlled benchmark systems. Conservation of the learned Hamiltonian under its exact continuous flow does not establish that it equals the physical energy, that a numerical solver conserves it exactly, or that the learned dynamics generalize outside the training conditions.

Handling dissipation, unknown coordinates, constraints, noisy measurements, and uncertainty remains a substantive modeling problem. Geometric integrators and learned dynamics address different parts of it. Neither replaces experiments or makes a simplified model universally valid.

Experimental mechanics also requires separating weak signals from other forces. Westphal, Hepach, Pfaff and Aspelmeyer (2021) measured gravitational coupling between gold spheres of approximately 1 mm radius and 90 mg using a torsion balance. Periodically moving the source mass produced a measurable signal at the test mass. The experiment connects torque, oscillators, calibration and uncertainty; it does not demonstrate quantum gravity. Alongside IAS15 gravitational integration and the double-pendulum study, it provides experimental and computational applications distinct from machine learning.

Limits and Connections

Newtonian mechanics is an approximation whose adequacy depends on the requested precision. Large size alone does not guarantee it. Relativistic effects matter at high speeds, and gravity may require spacetime curvature even for slowly moving bodies. Quantum effects matter when the relevant action and state preparation cannot be treated classically; comparing an action scale with \(\hbar\), the reduced Planck constant, is a guide rather than a complete criterion.

Deterministic equations do not promise exact practical prediction. Measurement uncertainty, chaotic sensitivity, unresolved interactions, and numerical error have different origins. Statistical methods can be necessary even when the underlying mechanical model is classical.

Technical Terms Glossary

  • ▣Reference frame: A system of axes and clocks used to specify position and time. An inertial frame gives constant velocity to a force-free body.
  • ▣Velocity and acceleration: Velocity is the rate of change of position; acceleration is the rate of change of velocity, including direction. Speed is the magnitude of velocity.
  • ▣Net force: The vector sum of forces acting on a chosen body. Forces on different bodies belong in different force diagrams.
  • ▣Constraint: A condition limiting allowed positions or velocities. Holonomic constraints can be expressed in coordinates and time.
  • ▣Work and power: Work transfers energy through force along displacement. Power measures the rate of energy transfer.
  • ▣Conservative force: A force whose work depends on endpoints rather than the path, within the domain considered; it admits a potential energy.
  • ▣Momentum and impulse: Newtonian momentum is mass times velocity. Impulse is force integrated over time and changes momentum.
  • ▣Center of mass: The mass-weighted mean position of a system. Its acceleration follows the total external force for constant total mass.
  • ▣Torque and angular momentum: Torque measures the turning effect of a force about an origin; it changes angular momentum about a fixed inertial origin.
  • ▣Moment of inertia: A measure of mass distribution relative to a rotation axis, weighted by squared perpendicular distance. The general three-dimensional relation uses a tensor.
  • ▣Centripetal: Directed toward the center of curvature; it describes the net-force component needed to bend a trajectory, not a separate interaction.
  • ▣Normal mode: A collective oscillation pattern of a linearized coupled system in which all parts oscillate at one mode frequency.
  • ▣Resonance: A large driven response near suitable natural frequencies; damping and nonlinear effects restrict the response.
  • ▣Degree of freedom: An independent coordinate needed to describe a configuration after applicable constraints are accounted for.
  • ▣Virtual displacement: An infinitesimal variation of configuration at fixed time consistent with the constraints, not an elapsed-time motion.
  • ▣Action and stationarity: Action is the time integral of the Lagrangian along a path. Stationarity makes its first variation zero, without requiring a minimum.
  • ▣Canonical momentum: The derivative of the Lagrangian with respect to a generalized velocity. It need not equal ordinary mass times velocity.
  • ▣Phase space: The space of coordinates and their conjugate momenta. A point specifies a mechanical state, not just a position in ordinary space.
  • ▣Legendre transform: The change from velocity variables to momentum variables used to construct a regular Hamiltonian from a Lagrangian.
  • ▣Symmetry: A transformation preserving the relevant mathematical description. Continuous action symmetries give conservation laws under Noether’s assumptions.
  • ▣Chaos: Sensitive dependence on initial conditions in deterministic dynamics; it limits prediction without making the laws random.
  • ▣Symplectic integrator: A numerical map preserving Hamiltonian phase-space geometry. This is not a guarantee of exact energy or negligible trajectory error.
  • ▣Convergence and stability: Convergence concerns approach to the solution as numerical resolution improves; stability concerns control of disturbances or numerical errors.
  • ▣Semimajor axis and eccentricity: Half the longest diameter of an ellipse, and its dimensionless elongation parameter. Zero eccentricity is a circle.
  • ▣Inertia tensor: Matrix describing how mass distribution converts angular-velocity components into angular-momentum components.
  • ▣Configuration space: All allowed choices of independent position coordinates; velocities or momenta are not yet specified.
  • ▣Phase and phase error: Phase locates a point in an oscillation cycle. Phase difference compares cycles; numerical phase error is the computed timing offset from the exact cycle.
  • ▣Holonomic and nonholonomic constraints: Holonomic constraints relate positions and time. Nonholonomic velocity restrictions cannot generally be reduced to position-only relations.
  • ▣Canonical transformation: Change of paired positions and momenta preserving Hamilton’s equation structure and phase-space geometry.
  • ▣Separable Hamiltonian: A sum of a momentum-only term and a position-only term, in the integration example used here.
  • ▣Linear and nonlinear equations: A linear equation combines the dependent variable and its derivatives to first power with coefficients independent of that variable. Products, powers or sine of that variable can make it nonlinear; nonlinearity alone does not imply chaos.
  • ▣Natural and driving frequency: The first belongs to free motion of the system; the second is imposed by an external periodic force. Angular frequencies use radians per second.
  • ▣Damping regimes: For a linear oscillator, underdamping retains oscillatory decay; critical damping is the boundary; overdamping gives nonoscillatory exponential decay.
  • ▣Transient and steady response: The transient retains the effect of initial conditions; with positive linear damping it decays, leaving the sustained driven response. Steady does not necessarily mean motionless.
  • ▣Adaptive integration: Numerical integration that adjusts timestep using an error estimate or tolerance; it requires a method compatible with that adjustment.
  • ▣Reduced mass: The product of two masses divided by their sum; it converts the isolated two-body problem into an equation for their relative separation.

Abbreviations and Unit Labels

  • ▣SI: International System of Units.
  • ▣CM: center of mass, used in the rolling-velocity subscript.
  • ▣m, s, kg: metre, second, kilogram. N, J: newton and joule. rad: radian, a dimensionless angle unit. A symbol such as m can instead denote mass within an equation; its local definition determines the meaning.
  • ▣DOI: Digital Object Identifier, a persistent identifier used in the references.
