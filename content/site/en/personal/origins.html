I was born in Mexico City. During my early childhood, my father, my mother, my younger brother, and I lived in a very small room in a shared housing complex. That room was our entire home. We had very little money and basic belongings: a bed, a table, and a radio/stereo that my mother used every day to listen to music.

I remember her listening to Héroes del Silencio, Mexican rock, and some American bands whose names I cannot recall. Music was part of our home. One of my earliest memories is of being carried in my father's arms, looking at him as I grew sleepy, and hearing piano music. I associate that memory with being a baby, although I cannot give it a precise age. I think the sound came from a radio, but I am not certain. The music changed abruptly to something I liked less, and then I fell asleep. Years later, through Somewhere in Time, I recognized the piano music as Rachmaninoff's Rhapsody on a Theme of Paganini, Op. 43. I had not known its name or composer when I heard it.

Both of my parents are very intelligent, and I have always admired how easily they understand how things work. They know how to take apart and repair engines, automobiles, motorcycles, electrical appliances, and many other things. I cannot do those repairs the way they can.

I was quiet and introverted, but I had a lot of energy and there was always something happening in my mind. I still recognize that combination in myself. Questions kept forming, especially at night. Smells, tastes, textures, physical sensations, and small details around me readily caught my attention. I was not deliberately trying to observe everything; those things simply drew my attention.

Among my toys, I especially loved my Hot Wheels cars and dinosaurs. With the cars, I was usually not inventing elaborate stories or staging races. I moved them with my hands and imagined driving along particular streets at an ordinary speed. I liked imagining the drive itself.

With the dinosaurs, I imagined them walking, looking around, and living in their surroundings. I imagined being them and living in their era. Much of my play involved experiencing movement or imagining what it would be like to inhabit another body, rather than making up a plot. Dinosaurs fascinated me, and documentaries, films, and other material about them have remained a strong interest.

I enjoyed being at home with my cousins and playing video games with them. Some of them had Nintendo consoles, so I had already played before we had a console of our own. My younger brother and I teased each other and had the usual sibling conflicts, but I was protective of him. When other children bullied him, I sometimes got into physical fights with them.

My father taught me to read when I was very young. When I was around five or six, my mother set me a challenge: read part of a book and explain what I had understood. There was also a cleaning chore to complete with my brother. I do not remember the book's title or which pages I read. If I completed the chore and explained the reading correctly, the reward would be a PlayStation. That was how we got our first PlayStation 1.

That was where my attachment to Tekken and Gran Turismo began, and it has stayed with me ever since. I enjoyed spending time with them and learning to play better. With Tekken, I began taking competition seriously at that age.

School was very different from being at home with my cousins. It was often miserable for me, beginning in kindergarten. I became bored quickly, and other children bullied me. Sometimes they took the food my parents had packed for me, on the days I had food to bring. Lessons felt scripted and disengaging to me, and I did not feel that the teachers were interested in teaching. That was how I experienced the classroom. I much preferred being at home playing with my cousins.

I also wondered what it felt like to be someone else. Did other people think in a voice inside their heads as I did? Did they wonder what being me felt like? Did food taste the same to them? Did they see the same purple I saw? These were questions that occupied me, along with why I existed, why anything existed, and how time worked. I cannot place them all at a particular age or remember a single moment when they began.

I cannot say when this began, but seeing or imagining physical touch can bring a sensation to my own body. If I imagine someone stroking a tree, for example, I can feel a corresponding sense of texture in my fingers. Seeing an injury can also affect me physically. Other people's emotions are different for me. I can notice someone's expression or behaviour, but seeing them happy or sad does not necessarily make me feel what they feel. I find it difficult to imagine what their emotional experience is like.

Home was both my safest place and the place of my worst nightmares. There was love, enjoyment, and familiarity there, alongside painful and frightening experiences. Both belong to my memories of those years.

My parents separated when I was approximately seven or eight. My younger brother and I remained with my father. That separation marked a change in our family life and the end of this early period of my childhood.
