Why I Study Fluids

Fluid mechanics and the Navier–Stokes problem have fascinated me for years. I have always been interested in liquids, gases, and other flowing media: how substances that are not rigid solids deform, move, and organize themselves, especially through turbulence and vortices. Whenever I have had time, I have studied Navier–Stokes on my own and worked through its equations.

Continuum Model and Conventions

A velocity field \(\mathbf u(x,t)=(u_1,u_2,u_3)\) assigns a velocity to each position \(x\) and time \(t\). Here the domain \(\Omega\) is either \(\mathbb R^3\), with sufficient decay at infinity, or the periodic torus \(\mathbb T^3=\mathbb R^3/\mathbb Z^3\). The fluid is Newtonian, incompressible, and has constant density \(\rho>0\). The pressure variable is the physical pressure divided by density, \(p=P/\rho\); \(\nu=\mu/\rho>0\) is kinematic viscosity, where \(\mu\) is dynamic viscosity; and \(\mathbf f\) is external force per unit mass. Thus every term in the momentum equation has units of acceleration.

This is a continuum description, not a molecular calculation. It excludes compressibility, free boundaries, and variable density. Gases can be treated as incompressible in appropriate regimes, but the equations here do not describe all gas flows. A mathematical singularity concerns this specified model; it is not a prediction that a real material literally reaches infinite speed.

Momentum, Transport, and Incompressibility

The local acceleration \(\partial_t\mathbf u\) measures change at a fixed spatial point. The nonlinear advection term has components \(((\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u)_i=\sum_j u_j\partial_{x_j}u_i\): the velocity transports itself through a nonuniform field. It can redistribute energy between positions and scales even though its contribution to total kinetic energy vanishes under the stated boundary conditions.

Along a material trajectory \(\dot X(t)=\mathbf u(X(t),t)\), the chain rule gives \(d\mathbf u(X(t),t)/dt=D\mathbf u/Dt\). This is the acceleration of a moving fluid element. The pressure force is \(-\nabla p\); viscous diffusion is \(\nu\Delta\mathbf u\), where \(\Delta=\sum_i\partial_{x_i}^2\); external forcing supplies \(\mathbf f\).

Incompressibility is the local volume-preservation constraint. General mass conservation is \(\partial_t\rho+\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf u)=0\); constant density reduces it to zero divergence. It does not require constant speed, zero deformation, or a fixed shape. A shrinking region selected by a velocity threshold or similarity coordinates need not contain the same fluid particles.

Taking divergence of momentum gives \(-\Delta p=\sum_{i,j}(\partial_{x_i}u_j)(\partial_{x_j}u_i)-\nabla\cdot\mathbf f\). Pressure is determined nonlocally by this elliptic constraint, up to the permitted time-dependent additive constant and the domain conditions. It is not a freely prescribed extra state variable.

Euler and the Role of Viscosity

Setting \(\nu=0\) gives the incompressible Euler equations. This is the formal inviscid system. Convergence of viscous solutions as \(\nu\downarrow0\) is a separate analytical question; boundaries and loss of regularity can obstruct a naive limit. At \(\nu>0\), the Fourier mode with wavevector \(k\) is damped by the linear diffusion factor \(e^{-\nu|k|^2t}\). Nonlinear transport simultaneously creates fine spatial structure. Removing viscosity changes the competition and the theorem being studied.

The Reynolds number \(\mathrm{Re}=UL/\nu\), for characteristic speed \(U\) and length \(L\), compares advective and viscous effects. A large value does not by itself prove turbulence or finite-time blowup. In an anisotropic flow there can be several relevant speeds and lengths, so one Reynolds number can hide important balances.

Vorticity and the Difference Between Two and Three Dimensions

Vorticity \(\boldsymbol\omega=\nabla\times\mathbf u\) measures local rotation; it is twice the angular velocity for rigid rotation. Curl removes the pressure gradient. The term \((\boldsymbol\omega\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u\) stretches and tilts vorticity. With strain tensor \(S=(\nabla\mathbf u+(\nabla\mathbf u)^{\mathsf T})/2\), its contribution to enstrophy is \(\int\boldsymbol\omega\cdot S\boldsymbol\omega\,dx\), which has no fixed sign. Enstrophy is \(\frac12\|\boldsymbol\omega\|_2^2\), and the superscript \(\mathsf T\) denotes matrix transpose.

$$\frac12\frac{d}{dt}\|\boldsymbol\omega\|_2^2+\nu\|\nabla\boldsymbol\omega\|_2^2=\int_\Omega\boldsymbol\omega\cdot S\boldsymbol\omega\,dx+\int_\Omega(\nabla\times\mathbf f)\cdot\boldsymbol\omega\,dx$$

For genuinely two-dimensional flow \(\mathbf u=(u_1(x_1,x_2),u_2(x_1,x_2),0)\), vorticity is perpendicular to the plane and \((\boldsymbol\omega\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u=0\). Its scalar equation is transport–diffusion with forcing curl. This removes the three-dimensional stretching term and gives much stronger a priori control; smooth 2D data with appropriate smooth forcing remain globally regular. A 3D axisymmetric flow with swirl is not a 2D flow in this sense.

Energy Control and Its Limits

Write \(\|v\|_q=(\int_\Omega|v|^q\,dx)^{1/q}\) for \(1\leq q<\infty\), and \(\|v\|_\infty\) for the essential supremum. With density normalized to one, kinetic energy is \(E(t)=\frac12\|\mathbf u(t)\|_2^2\). Multiplication of momentum by \(\mathbf u\), integration, and zero divergence remove transport and pressure from the global balance. For smooth, sufficiently decaying or periodic solutions:

$$\frac{dE}{dt}+\nu\|\nabla\mathbf u\|_2^2=\int_\Omega\mathbf f\cdot\mathbf u\,dx$$

The second formula is the Leray–Hopf energy inequality, valid from \(s=0\) and almost every admissible starting time \(s\), for later times using the usual weakly continuous representative. Here \(a\) is the integration time. The inequality permits a loss in the limiting energy balance. It does not permit spontaneous gain without input from the force.

The bounds \(\mathbf u\in L^\infty_tL^2_x\) and \(\nabla\mathbf u\in L^2_tL^2_x\) control total energy and accumulated dissipation on finite intervals. They do not control \(\|\mathbf u\|_\infty\) or all derivatives. A field of amplitude \(A\) supported in volume \(V\) can have energy of order \(A^2V\); \(A\) may diverge while \(V\) decreases fast enough to keep that product bounded. In three dimensions, \(H^1\) embeds into \(L^6\), not \(L^\infty\), and the available gradient bound is time-integrated.

Existence, Weak Solutions, and Regularity

Existence means finding fields satisfying the equations, initial data, boundary conditions, and a specified solution class. Regularity specifies integrability, continuity, or differentiability. Smoothness means \(C^\infty\), with continuous derivatives of all orders on the domain under discussion. A strong solution has enough regularity to satisfy the evolution almost everywhere and support the relevant uniqueness estimates; the precise function space must be stated. For example, with suitable data, \(C([0,T];H^1)\cap L^2(0,T;H^2)\) is a standard strong velocity class. Strong and smooth are not interchangeable definitions.

For integer \(m\geq0\), \(H^m(\Omega)\) is the Sobolev space of square-integrable fields with square-integrable weak spatial derivatives through order \(m\). The notation \(L^p_tL^q_x\) means taking the spatial \(L^q\) norm first, then the temporal \(L^p\) norm; an infinite exponent uses the essential supremum. Weak continuity in \(L^2\) means that the pairing with each fixed \(L^2\) test field is continuous in time.

A weak solution satisfies integrated equations against smooth test functions rather than requiring pointwise classical derivatives. For every divergence-free \(\boldsymbol\varphi\in C_c^\infty(\Omega\times[0,T);\mathbb R^3)\), with periodic spatial tests on the torus:

The tensor product has entries \((\mathbf u\otimes\mathbf u)_{ij}=u_i u_j\); \(A:B=\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}B_{ij}\); and \((\nabla\boldsymbol\varphi)_{ij}=\partial_j\varphi_i\). Integration by parts transfers derivatives to the test field. Pressure drops out because \(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\varphi=0\). The initial term encodes \(\mathbf u_0\). Zero divergence must also hold in distributions.

A Leray–Hopf solution is more than an arbitrary distributional solution: it lies in the energy class, is weakly continuous in \(L^2\), attains the initial data, and satisfies the energy inequality. Global construction in this class does not show that the solution is smooth or unique. A suitable weak solution additionally satisfies a local energy inequality involving pressure; this is the class used in partial regularity theory.

Local smooth solutions exist for sufficiently regular compatible data. A finite maximal smooth lifespan \(T_*\) means smooth continuation fails at that time. A singularity is loss of the required regularity, not merely a rapidly changing picture or an under-resolved numerical grid. With smooth forcing, a bound strong enough for a continuation theorem would prevent such breakdown. OpenAI’s stated example has the explicit stronger diagnostic \(\limsup_{t\uparrow1}\|\mathbf u(t)\|_\infty=\infty\).

Scaling and Critical Quantities

For \(\lambda>0\) on \(\mathbb R^3\), these transformations preserve the equation with the same \(\nu\), on the correspondingly rescaled time interval and with rescaled initial data. A change of variables gives

$$\|\mathbf u_\lambda\|_{L^p_tL^q_x}=\lambda^{1-2/p-3/q}\|\mathbf u\|_{L^p_tL^q_x},\qquad\|\mathbf u_\lambda(t)\|_2^2=\lambda^{-1}\|\mathbf u(\lambda^2t)\|_2^2$$

A norm is scale-critical when its exponent is zero: \(2/p+3/q=1\). Examples are \(L^\infty_tL^3_x\) and the spatial homogeneous Sobolev norm \(\dot H^{1/2}\). The homogeneous Sobolev norm \(\dot H^s\) measures the Fourier transform of the field with squared weight \(|k|^{2s}\), where \(k\) is spatial frequency. In \(\dot H^s\), the norm scales as \(\lambda^{s-1/2}\). Here \(p,q\) are integrability exponents, not the pressure. On a fixed unit torus, arbitrary rescaling is not a global symmetry; the same scaling still diagnoses local concentration.

The energy norm is supercritical in the regularity problem: concentrating a fixed profile with \(\lambda\to\infty\) makes its \(L^2\) norm smaller, even while pointwise speed and gradients grow. Energy control therefore becomes weaker at fine scales. For \(q>3\), suitable finite \(L^p_tL^q_x\) control with \(2/p+3/q\leq1\) gives a classical regularity criterion under its standard hypotheses. The endpoint \(L^\infty_tL^3_x\) requires the deeper backward-uniqueness argument of Escauriaza–Seregin–Šverák. Their notation \(L_{3,\infty}\) denotes this mixed space; it must not be confused with the spatial weak-Lorentz space \(L^{3,\infty}_x\).

What the Clay Alternatives Actually Ask

The official formulation by Charles L. Fefferman asks for a proof of one of four statements, for positive viscosity in three dimensions:

  • A: Every smooth divergence-free initial velocity on \(\mathbb R^3\), with all derivatives rapidly decaying at spatial infinity, admits a global smooth solution with uniformly bounded energy when \(\mathbf f=0\).
  • B: Every smooth divergence-free periodic initial velocity admits a global smooth spatially periodic solution when \(\mathbf f=0\).
  • C: There exist smooth divergence-free initial data and smooth forcing on \(\mathbb R^3\), satisfying the prescribed rapid decay of all derivatives, for which no global smooth solution with uniformly bounded energy exists.
  • D: There exist smooth periodic divergence-free initial data and smooth periodic forcing, with all forcing derivatives rapidly decaying in time, for which no global smooth periodic solution exists.

The whole-space decay requirement for forcing is \(|\partial_x^\alpha\partial_t^m\mathbf f(x,t)|\leq C_{\alpha m N}(1+|x|+t)^{-N}\) for every multi-index \(\alpha\) and nonnegative integers \(m,N\). The periodic version replaces the right-hand weight by \((1+t)^{-N}\). Thus C/D allow forcing, whereas A/B prescribe zero forcing. These are not paired logical negations with identical hypotheses. A forced counterexample does not by itself supply an unforced counterexample.

Research Lineage: Distinct Theorems, Distinct Hypotheses

Jean Leray (1934)

Sur le mouvement d’un liquide visqueux emplissant l’espace. Acta Mathematica 63, 193–248.

Constructed global finite-energy weak solutions. Compactness and energy estimates provide existence in a generalized class, but do not control every derivative or establish global smoothness.

Luis Caffarelli, Robert Kohn, Louis Nirenberg (1982)

Partial regularity of suitable weak solutions of the Navier–Stokes equations. Communications on Pure and Applied Mathematics 35(6), 771–831.

For suitable weak solutions under the theorem’s forcing hypotheses, the singular set has zero one-dimensional parabolic Hausdorff measure. Parabolic geometry weights time like length squared. This severely restricts possible singularities; it does not show that the singular set is empty.

Luis Escauriaza, Gregory Seregin, Vladimír Šverák (2003)

\(L_{3,\infty}\)-solutions of the Navier–Stokes equations and backward uniqueness. Russian Mathematical Surveys 58(2), 211–250.

Established endpoint regularity from \(L^\infty_tL^3_x\) control for the Cauchy problem. A contradiction argument combines rescaling, limiting solutions, and backward uniqueness for a parabolic equation; mere bounded energy is not this critical control.

Terence Tao (2016)

Finite time blowup for an averaged three-dimensional Navier–Stokes equation. Journal of the American Mathematical Society 29(3), 601–674.

Constructed blowup for an averaged modification of the nonlinear operator that retains an energy cancellation. This identifies limits of arguments using only broad energy and size estimates. The operator is modified: this is not a blowup proof for the original Navier–Stokes system. The journal volume is 2016; publication online began in 2015.

Tristan Buckmaster, Vlad Vicol (2019)

Nonuniqueness of weak solutions to the Navier–Stokes equation. Annals of Mathematics 189(1), 101–144.

Used convex integration and intermittent oscillations to produce nonunique finite-energy weak solutions on the torus. This weak class does not impose the full Leray–Hopf energy admissibility conditions. Nonuniqueness in it is distinct from loss of smoothness of the unique local classical solution.

Dallas Albritton, Elia Brué, Maria Colombo (2022)

Non-uniqueness of Leray solutions of the forced Navier–Stokes equations. Annals of Mathematics 196(1), 415–455.

Produced distinct Leray solutions from zero initial velocity with the same force. An unstable self-similar background uses a compactly supported vortex-ring profile; another solution follows an unstable direction in similarity variables. The force belongs to \(L^1_tL^2_x\) but is singular at the initial time. The work demonstrates how vortex instability can drive genuine nonuniqueness in an energy-admissible class, not smooth-forcing blowup of the later OpenAI type.

Diego Córdoba, Luis Martínez-Zoroa (2023)

Blow-up for the incompressible 3D-Euler equations with uniform \(C^{1,\frac12-\epsilon}\cap L^2\) force. arXiv:2309.08495.

A forced, non-axisymmetric Euler construction with force uniformly controlled in the stated Hölder and energy spaces. Successive vortex structures drive loss of regularity. Euler has no viscous Laplacian; the forcing is not asserted to satisfy Clay’s smoothness assumptions. The verified citation here is the 2023 arXiv preprint.

Diego Córdoba, Luis Martínez-Zoroa, Fan Zheng (2026)

Finite Time Blow-Up for the Hypodissipative Navier Stokes Equations with a Force in \(L^1_t C_x^{1,\epsilon}\cap L^\infty_t L^2_x\). Archive for Rational Mechanics and Analysis 250, article 38.

Replaces full viscosity by \(\nu|\nabla|^\alpha=\nu(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}\), for \(0\leq\alpha<(22-8\sqrt7)/9\). Classical finite-energy solutions develop a singularity with force in the title’s class. Positive \(\alpha\) introduces dissipation, and controlling its effect was an important step beyond Euler. Ordinary Navier–Stokes has \(\alpha=2\), far outside this range; this theorem does not reach that case.

OpenAI (2026): Statement and Status

OpenAI published Finite Time Blowup for Navier–Stokes on September 8, 2026, claiming a finite-time blowup construction for the forced three-dimensional incompressible system, together with a Lean formalization. The paper credits OpenAI. Paper · Official publication · Lean repository.

Status checked September 13, 2026: Clay’s problem page still labels the problem active. This page explains the published theorem and construction; it does not assert universal acceptance or a prize decision. The repository provides formal statements and build instructions. Linking it is not a claim that this website independently audited or rebuilt the formal proof.

Theorem 1.1, stated scope. For every \(\nu>0\), the paper asserts the existence of a force \(\mathbf f\in C_c^\infty(\mathbb R^3\times(0,\infty);\mathbb R^3)\), a compact set \(K\subset\mathbb R^3\), and smooth \(\mathbf u,p\) on \(\mathbb R^3\times[0,1)\), satisfying momentum, incompressibility, and \(\mathbf u(\cdot,0)=0\), with

$$\begin{gathered}\operatorname{supp}\mathbf u(\cdot,t)\cup\operatorname{supp}p(\cdot,t)\subset K\quad(0\leq t<1),\\ \sup_{0\leq t<1}\|\mathbf u(t)\|_{L^2(\mathbb R^3)}<\infty,\qquad\limsup_{t\uparrow1}\|\mathbf u(t)\|_{L^\infty(\mathbb R^3)}=\infty.\end{gathered}$$

The notation \(C_c^\infty\) means infinitely differentiable with compact support in space and time. In particular the force is zero near \(t=0\) and is smooth through \(t=1\); it is not allowed to become singular when the velocity does. The fixed set \(K\) contains the velocity and pressure supports before blowup. Uniform boundedness of \(\|\mathbf u\|_2\) gives uniformly bounded kinetic energy, whereas the last condition is unbounded speed along times approaching one.

The stated consequence is nonexistence of a global smooth solution with the same force and zero initial data and uniformly bounded total energy. Local uniqueness rules out an alternative smooth evolution that simply avoids the constructed singularity. This gives the paper’s claimed alternative C. Spatial localization and rescaling permit periodization on \(\mathbb R^3/\mathbb Z^3\), yielding its alternative D (Corollary 10.6). These claims retain positive viscosity and nonzero forcing.

Geometry of the Concentrating Core

Use cylindrical coordinates \(x=(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta,z)\). The leading field is axisymmetric: its components \(u_r^{(0)},u_\theta^{(0)},u_z^{(0)}\) are independent of \(\theta\). It has inward radial flow and azimuthal rotation near the core, with axial outflow on either side of a dividing plane close to \(z=0\). Fluid can spiral inward and leave along the axis. The axial extent of the concentrating region nevertheless shrinks: that region is not a material parcel. Farther from the central plane, radial outflow can also occur.

Let \(\tau=1-t\), \(A=\frac12+h\), and \(D=\frac12-h\), with fixed \(0<h<1/100\). The paper’s similarity coordinates are

$$\tau=q(1-\eta^2),\qquad z=q^D\eta,\qquad X=\frac{r^2}{2q},\qquad q>0,\quad |\eta|<1$$

Here \(q=q(z,\tau)\) is an auxiliary scale variable, not an integrability exponent; \(X\) is a rescaled squared radius, and \(\eta\) is a rescaled axial coordinate. Eliminating \(\eta\) gives \(q-z^2q^{2h}=\tau\). Its relevant derivative is \(1-2h\eta^2>0\), selecting a unique scale. In a core with bounded \(X\) and \(|\eta|\leq\eta_c<1\), \(q\asymp\tau\). The leading profiles satisfy \(u_\theta^{(0)}=q^{-A}E(X,\eta)\), \(u_z^{(0)}=q^{-A}U(X,\eta)\), \(ru_r^{(0)}=V_0(X,\eta)\), and \(p^{(0)}=q^{-2A}\Pi(X,\eta)\). The symbols \(E,U,V_0,\Pi\) here are profile functions; \(E\) is not kinetic energy in this formula.

Here \(U_i\) denotes the characteristic supremum of the corresponding leading velocity component over the core, and \(\asymp\) means upper and lower bounds by fixed positive multiples. These are not pointwise equalities: azimuthal velocity vanishes on the axis. Both lengths tend to zero, but radial collapse is faster. The aspect ratio \(\ell_z/\ell_r\asymp\tau^{-h}\) diverges slowly. The core volume is \(O(\tau^{3/2-h})\); multiplying by squared characteristic speed gives \(E_{\mathrm{core}}=O(\tau^{1/2-3h})\to0\). This core estimate is distinct from the theorem’s global energy bound.

At fixed viscosity, \(\mathrm{Re}_\theta\asymp\tau^{-h}\) while the radial Reynolds number stays \(O(1)\), with constants depending on the construction. Radial diffusion remains in the leading balance. Axial diffusion is smaller relative to radial diffusion by \(\ell_r^2/\ell_z^2\asymp q^{2h}\), the small parameter for successive background corrections. The full field also contains non-axisymmetric pulses: axisymmetry describes the leading background, not the completed solution.

Momentum Flux and Oscillatory Pulses

The leading vortex alone does not solve the full equations. At viscosity one, define the momentum residual \(\mathcal R(\mathbf u,p)=\partial_t\mathbf u+(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u-\Delta\mathbf u+\nabla p\). For a divergence-free correction \(\mathbf w\) and pressure correction \(\pi\), the exact algebraic identity is

$$\begin{aligned}\mathcal R(\mathbf u_B+\mathbf w,p_B+\pi)&=\mathcal R(\mathbf u_B,p_B)\\ &\quad+\partial_t\mathbf w+(\mathbf u_B\cdot\nabla)\mathbf w\\ &\quad+(\mathbf w\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u_B-\Delta\mathbf w\\ &\quad+\nabla\pi+\nabla\cdot(\mathbf w\otimes\mathbf w)\end{aligned}$$

The subscript \(B\) denotes the corrected background. Oscillations can have zero angular mean velocity but nonzero mean quadratic products. For a frozen local wave \(\mathbf w=\mathbf a\cos(\xi\cdot x+\phi)\), with \(\mathbf a\cdot\xi=0\), its phase mean is zero while \(\langle\mathbf w\otimes\mathbf w\rangle=\mathbf a\otimes\mathbf a/2\). Here \(\mathbf a\) is amplitude, \(\xi\) is wavevector, and \(\phi\) is phase. Localized waves require additional divergence corrections; this elementary wave is only an explanation of the quadratic flux.

The construction places two families of spatially oscillatory pulses in an annulus. Their averaged radial fluxes of azimuthal and axial momentum are selected to supply the missing stress. The desired two-component stress must lie inside a positive cone generated by the two covariance directions, so its coefficients can be realized as squared amplitudes. This compatibility is part of the profile construction, not an arbitrary choice made after it.

Tiny seeds are amplified by background shear. Shear also changes wave orientation and shortens radial wavelengths until viscous damping dominates. Each pulse grows and then decays; the cutoff tails must be small in every derivative. Ignoring logarithmic factors and boundary weights, the paper’s outline gives pulse amplitude \(A_{\mathrm{wave}}\asymp q^{-1/2-h/2}\) and wavelength \(\ell_{\mathrm{wave}}\asymp q^{1/2+h/2}\). Their ratios to background speed and radial length are both of order \(q^{h/2}\). The squared pulse amplitude, divided by the radial scale, is of order \(q^{-3/2-h}\), matching the needed stress divergence. These estimates describe the construction’s hierarchy, not a numerical resolution prescription.

Matching, Corrections, and Smooth Forcing

The inner profiles satisfy the leading equations near the axis. The outer local flow is purely azimuthal and satisfies an exact radial heat equation. Between them, matching cumulative radial moments preserves pressure, radial velocity, and stress constraints. In particular, vanishing weighted integrals of the tangential residual make the stress vanish outside the pulse annulus. Matching just the pointwise values of velocity would not enforce these integral conditions.

Corrections in successive powers of \(q^{2h}\) account for axial viscosity and lower-order background terms. Vector potentials allow localization while retaining zero divergence: taking a curl after applying a cutoff differs fundamentally from multiplying velocity itself by a cutoff. Pulse interactions then generate both oscillatory and mean errors; further compactly supported corrections improve the residual to arbitrarily high order.

The central requirement is not that acceleration, pressure, transport, and diffusion be separately bounded. Their singular contributions must cancel in the sum defining \(\mathcal R\). The remaining errors are made flat at the singularity: every Cartesian space-time derivative decays faster than any prescribed power of the small scale in the relevant regions. Away from the singular point, the inner/outer construction has smooth limits, so the final spatial and temporal localization generates only smooth additional forcing. This is what allows \(\mathbf f=\mathcal R\) to extend smoothly through the singular time.

The geometric scaling, admissible stress, pulse growth and damping, moment matching, and all-order error control are separate parts of the argument. A vortex drawing or the energy scaling alone establishes none of those compatibility estimates. The 166-page paper develops them in Sections 4–10 and Appendices A–C.

Geometry and the Analytical Result

The distinction matters: energy scaling can explain compatibility with a singularity, while the analytical construction must establish the equations, smooth forcing, support properties, and continuation obstruction simultaneously. The primary sources above supply those claims and their precise scopes.
