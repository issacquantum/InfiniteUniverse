Time-Dependent Schrödinger Equation

Equation

$$i\hbar \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Psi(\mathbf{r}, t) = \hat{H}\Psi(\mathbf{r}, t)$$

The same evolution law can be written without choosing position coordinates:

$$i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}|\psi(t)\rangle=\hat H|\psi(t)\rangle$$

The ket form describes an abstract state. For a single spinless particle, taking its position representation gives the wavefunction form above. A many-particle wavefunction instead depends on all particle coordinates.

Literal Reading

“i times h-bar times the partial derivative with respect to t of capital psi at vector r and t equals H-hat acting on capital psi at vector r and t.” In the second expression, replace the wavefunction with “ket psi of t.” The vertical bar and closing angle bracket together denote a ket, not an absolute value. The hat identifies an operator.

Common Reading

The Hamiltonian tells how the quantum state changes with time. This equation does not, by itself, say that the state has one definite energy.

How a Physicist Interprets It

Specify the system, its Hamiltonian, an initial state and the domain and boundary conditions on which the Hamiltonian acts. Evolution then predicts amplitudes at later times; measurement probabilities require the Born rule. A self-adjoint Hamiltonian generates unitary evolution under the usual existence conditions. Position coordinates label amplitudes, not a classical particle trajectory.

Symbols

  • \(i\): dimensionless imaginary unit; its square is minus one. Multiplication by it changes complex phase.
  • \(\hbar\): reduced Planck constant, with units joule seconds (J s). The bar is part of its name, not subtraction.
  • \(\partial/\partial t\): rate of change with respect to time while position is held fixed; it introduces a factor with units s⁻¹. In the ket form, there is only a time parameter, so an ordinary derivative is equivalent.
  • \(t\): time in seconds; \(\mathbf r\): position vector in metres. Bold type identifies a spatial vector, and parentheses specify function arguments.
  • \(\Psi(\mathbf r,t)\): complex probability-amplitude field. For a single particle normalized over three-dimensional volume, its units are \(\mathrm{m}^{-3/2}\). Its squared modulus is a probability density, not a probability at one point.
  • \(|\psi(t)\rangle\): normalized state vector in Hilbert space. Its norm is dimensionless; it is not a spatial vector in metres.
  • \(\hat H\): Hamiltonian operator, with energy units J. Acting on a state is an operator operation, not ordinary multiplication by a measured energy.
  • The equals sign equates the full state-valued expressions. In the wavefunction form, both sides have units J \(\mathrm{m}^{-3/2}\); in the ket form, both have units of energy times a dimensionless state.

Values and Example

Planck’s constant is exactly \(h=6.62607015\times10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\,s}\). Dividing by the dimensionless number \(2\pi\) defines \(\hbar=h/(2\pi)=1.054571817\ldots\times10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\,s}\). The definition is exact; a finite decimal is rounded. The Hamiltonian and initial state have no universal numerical values: they depend on the system and its preparation. See the NIST constants table below.

For a time-independent Hamiltonian and an energy eigenstate, the solution is \(|\psi(t)\rangle=e^{-iEt/\hbar}|\psi(0)\rangle\). Read this as “ket psi at t equals the exponential of minus i E t over h-bar times ket psi at zero.” Here \(E\) is that state’s energy in joules, \(e\) is the dimensionless exponential base, and zero labels the initial time. The exponent is dimensionless. An illustrative energy of 1 electronvolt, or exactly \(1.602176634\times10^{-19}\) J, gives a phase change of about −1.52 radians after \(10^{-15}\) s. This phase is not a measurable change in the position density of that single eigenstate; relative phases between different energies can affect observables.

Meaning of the Equation

Differentiate the exponential in the example: it contributes minus i times E divided by h-bar. Multiplication by i h-bar then gives E times the state, matching the action of the Hamiltonian on an energy eigenstate. A superposition can contain several such energies and phases. Finding the energy eigenstates is a separate stationary eigenvalue problem; the time-dependent equation evolves the full state, whether or not it is an eigenstate.

Scope and Limits

This is quantum state evolution with time supplied as a parameter. The position form here uses a nonrelativistic particle description. A subsystem interacting with an untracked environment generally needs a density operator and reduced dynamics rather than this closed pure-state equation. The equation neither selects a measurement outcome nor establishes a metaphysical interpretation. A prescribed time-dependent Hamiltonian is allowed, but the simple fixed-energy exponential used in the example is then generally inapplicable.
