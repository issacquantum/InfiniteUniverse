Hydrostatic Equilibrium and Stellar Structure

Equation

$$\frac{dP}{dr}=-\frac{G M(r)\rho(r)}{r^2}$$

Literal reading

The derivative of P with respect to r equals minus G times M of r times rho of r, divided by r squared.

Symbols and units

\(P(r)\) is total isotropic pressure in pascals (Pa); \(r\) is distance from the center in meters (m); \(M(r)\) is the mass enclosed within that radius in kilograms (kg); \(\rho(r)\) is local mass density in kg/m³; and \(G\) is the gravitational constant, approximately 6.67430 × 10⁻¹¹ m³ kg⁻¹ s⁻². The derivative \(dP/dr\) is the outward rate of pressure change in Pa/m. The minus sign means pressure decreases outward where density and enclosed mass are positive.

Physical meaning

At each radius, the outward pressure-gradient force per unit volume balances the inward gravitational force per unit volume. Dividing their magnitudes by density gives the corresponding accelerations. The pressure decreases outward; pressure is not itself a directed force.

Assumptions and limits

The equation applies at positive radius; at a regular center, its limit is taken as the enclosed mass tends to zero. This radial equation assumes spherical symmetry, negligible bulk acceleration, and negligible rotational and magnetic forces. It does not by itself determine a star: an equation of state, mass conservation, energy generation and transport, and boundary conditions are also needed. Strongly relativistic stars require a relativistic equilibrium equation.

Worked example

For an illustrative layer with enclosed mass 1.0 × 10³⁰ kg, radius 1.0 × 10⁸ m, and density 1.0 × 10³ kg/m³, the required pressure gradient is approximately −6.67430 × 10⁶ Pa/m. Across 100 m, pressure drops by about 6.67430 × 10⁸ Pa if those quantities vary negligibly over the layer. These values illustrate the local balance, not a complete solar model.
