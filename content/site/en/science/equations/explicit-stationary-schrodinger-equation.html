Explicit Stationary Schrödinger Equation

Equation

$$-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2\psi(\mathbf{r}) + V(\mathbf{r})\psi(\mathbf{r}) = E\psi(\mathbf{r})$$

Literal Reading

“Minus h-bar squared divided by twice m, times the Laplacian of psi at vector r, plus V at vector r times psi at vector r, equals E times psi at vector r.” The first minus sign belongs to the kinetic-energy operator; there are no brackets around the potential in this displayed form.

Common Reading

For an energy eigenstate, the kinetic-energy operator and the potential-energy term together give the total energy multiplied by the wavefunction.

How a Physicist Interprets It

Specify the particle mass, a static scalar potential energy and boundary conditions. Solve for nonzero, physically admissible wavefunctions and their energies. Spatial curvature enters the kinetic term, so changing the confinement or the potential changes the allowed states. This is the position-space energy eigenvalue problem for the particular Hamiltonian written here, not a formula for every quantum system.

Symbols

  • \(\hbar\): reduced Planck constant, in J s; its superscript 2 means its square. The numerical 2 in the denominator is dimensionless.
  • \(m\): constant particle mass in kg. It is a scalar model parameter, not the metre unit appearing in dimensional statements.
  • \(\nabla^2\): Laplacian. In Cartesian coordinates it is the sum of the second partial derivatives with respect to x, y and z. The superscript 2 denotes this second-order differential operator, not the square of the value of a function. It contributes units m⁻².
  • \(\mathbf r\): position vector in metres; \(\psi(\mathbf r)\): spatial wavefunction, with units \(\mathrm{m}^{-3/2}\) for a normalized three-dimensional bound state. Parentheses specify position dependence.
  • \(V(\mathbf r)\): scalar potential energy in J, multiplying the wavefunction at that position; it is not electric potential in volts. \(E\): scalar energy eigenvalue in J.
  • The plus sign adds two functions; the equals sign holds at every position in the domain. Each term has units J times the units of the wavefunction. The initial minus sign is part of the kinetic operator and does not make kinetic energy negative.

Values and Example

\(\hbar=1.054571817\ldots\times10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\,s}\), defined exactly as Planck’s constant divided by two pi. The NIST 2022 electron mass is \(9.1093837139(28)\times10^{-31}\) kg; the parentheses give the standard uncertainty in the last digits. Potential energy, eigenenergy and mass are chosen for the physical system; only the quoted fundamental constants have universal values.

Consider the one-dimensional reduction for an electron in an ideal well of width L = 1 nm: the potential is zero inside, and the wavefunction vanishes at both ends. The ground-state shape is a sine with one half-wave across the well. Its second derivative is minus pi squared divided by L squared times the same sine. Substitution gives \(E=\hbar^2\pi^2/(2mL^2)\), read “E equals h-bar squared times pi squared divided by twice m times L squared.” Here pi is the dimensionless circle constant and L is the well width in metres. This gives about \(6.02\times10^{-20}\) J, or 0.376 electronvolts. Doubling the width divides this energy by four.

Meaning of the Equation

Inside the well the potential term vanishes. Applying the second derivative to the sine produces a negative factor; the minus sign in the kinetic operator cancels it, leaving a positive energy. The zero values at the boundaries restrict the sine wavelengths. This example explains discrete confinement energies; it is not evidence that every potential has a discrete spectrum.

Scope and Limits

Single nonrelativistic particle with constant mass, a time-independent scalar potential energy and no vector-potential coupling or spin terms. The Laplacian must use the chosen geometry, not an unmodified Cartesian formula in curvilinear coordinates. Bound states require normalization and appropriate boundary behavior; scattering states need different boundary and normalization conventions. The infinite well is an idealization, not an exact description of a real material barrier.
