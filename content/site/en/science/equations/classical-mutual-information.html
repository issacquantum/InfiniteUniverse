Classical Mutual Information

Equation

$$ I(X;Y)=H(X)+H(Y)-H(X,Y) $$

Literal Reading

I of X;Y equals H of X plus H of Y minus H of X comma Y.

Common Reading

The amount of uncertainty removed about one variable when the other variable is known.

Symbols

\(I(X;Y)\): mutual information between random variables \(X\) and \(Y\).

\(H(X)\) and \(H(Y)\): individual Shannon entropies.

\(H(X,Y)\): joint entropy of the pair.

Meaning of the Equation

Mutual information detects statistical dependence between variables. It does not by itself say which variable causes the other or whether an external common cause exists.

Conditions and units

For discrete variables, the sum runs over pairs with nonzero joint probability. Terms with zero joint probability contribute zero. Base-two logarithms give bits; natural logarithms give nats. The entropy identity assumes the entropies involved are finite.

Worked example or interpretation

Let X be a fair bit and Y an exact copy. Each individual entropy is 1 bit, while the pair has only two equally likely possibilities, so its joint entropy is 1 bit. Mutual information is 1+1−1=1 bit. If the two fair bits were independent, the joint entropy would be 2 bits and mutual information zero.

Notation details

The semicolon separates the two variables whose information is compared; it is not conditioning. The comma inside H(X,Y) denotes the joint variable, not multiplication.

Source

Claude E. Shannon, A Mathematical Theory of Communication.
