Trace Distance

Equation

$$ D(\rho,\sigma)=\frac{1}{2}\|\rho-\sigma\|_1 $$

Literal Reading

D of rho and sigma equals one half times the trace norm of rho minus sigma.

Common Reading

The operational distinguishability between two quantum states.

Symbols

\(D(\rho,\sigma)\): the trace distance between two states.

\(\rho,\sigma\): density matrices being compared.

\(\|\cdot\|_1\): the trace norm, equal to the sum of singular values.

\(\tfrac{1}{2}\): the normalization that keeps the distance between zero and one for quantum states.

Meaning of the Equation

Trace distance is operational: it tells the best possible advantage for distinguishing two quantum states with a measurement. Small distance means the states are nearly indistinguishable in any experiment.

Conditions and units

The density matrices are normalized and positive, so D is dimensionless and lies in [0,1]. For equal prior probabilities and one copy, optimal discrimination succeeds with probability \((1+D)/2\); the advantage over random guessing is D/2. Small single-copy distance does not guarantee indistinguishability after arbitrarily many independent copies. The trace norm of a Hermitian difference is the sum of absolute eigenvalues.

Reference

John Watrous, The Theory of Quantum Information.

Worked example or interpretation

For diagonal qubit states with populations (0.7,0.3) and (0.4,0.6), their difference has eigenvalues 0.3 and −0.3. The trace norm is 0.6, so trace distance is 0.3. With equal prior probabilities, optimal discrimination succeeds with probability (1+0.3)/2=0.65.
