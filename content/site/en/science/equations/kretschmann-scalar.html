Kretschmann Scalar

Equation

$$K = R_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}R^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma} = \frac{48G^2M^2}{c^4r^6}$$

Literal Reading

K equals R subscript mu nu rho sigma times R superscript mu nu rho sigma, and also forty-eight G squared M squared divided by c to the fourth times r to the sixth.

Common Reading

The Kretschmann scalar measures curvature strength independent of coordinates.

Symbols

\(K\): Kretschmann scalar.

\(R_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\): Riemann curvature tensor.

\(M\): black hole mass.

\(r\): radial coordinate.

Meaning of the Equation

The true curvature singularity is at \(r=0\); the Schwarzschild horizon is not a curvature singularity.

Scope and Limits

This scalar is coordinate independent, so it separates a coordinate problem from a real curvature blowup. It still belongs to classical general relativity and is expected to fail where quantum gravity becomes essential.

Conditions and units

The last equality is specifically the Schwarzschild vacuum solution, with areal radius \(r\) in m and mass \(M\) in kg. \(G\) has units m³ kg⁻¹ s⁻² and \(c\) m/s, giving \(K\) units m⁻⁴. All four repeated spacetime indices are contracted using the metric. A divergent invariant establishes curvature blowup, but a finite or zero Kretschmann scalar alone does not exclude every kind of singularity in an arbitrary spacetime.

Equivalent notation

$$K=\frac{48G^2M^2}{c^4r^6}$$This is the Schwarzschild value of the contraction displayed above: K equals forty-eight G squared M squared divided by c to the fourth r to the sixth.

Worked example or interpretation

At the Schwarzschild horizon r=rs, substitution gives K=12/rs⁴, finite for nonzero mass. Doubling r at fixed mass divides this invariant by 64. The divergence at r=0 is different from the coordinate singularity at the horizon.

Source

Sean M. Carroll, Lecture Notes on General Relativity.

Physical constants
