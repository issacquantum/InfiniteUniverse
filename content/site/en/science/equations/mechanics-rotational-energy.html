Angular Momentum and Rotational Energy

Equation

$$\mathbf L=\mathbf r\times\mathbf p,\quad I=\sum_i m_i r_{\perp i}^2,\quad K_{\mathrm{rot}}=\tfrac12I\omega^2$$

Literal Reading

Vector L equals vector r cross vector p. I is the sum of mass i times perpendicular distance i squared. Rotational energy is one half I times omega squared.

Common Reading

Angular momentum depends on the origin, and rotational energy on the mass distribution about the axis.

Symbols

  • \(\mathbf r,\mathbf p,\mathbf L\): particle position (m), momentum (kg m/s), angular momentum (kg m²/s). \(\times\): right-hand-rule cross product. \(i,\sum_i,m_i,r_{\perp i}\): particle index, sum over particles, mass (kg), perpendicular distance from the axis (m). \(I,K_{\mathrm{rot}},\omega\): inertia (kg m²), rotational energy (J), angular speed (rad/s); rot means rotation.

Meaning of the Equation

For a particle, \(\mathbf L=\mathbf r\times\mathbf p\), in kg m²/s. The position vector starts at the chosen origin, so changing that origin can change angular momentum. A particle moving directly toward the origin has zero angular momentum about it even with nonzero momentum. For a rigid body, \(I=\sum_i m_i r_{\perp i}^{2}\), where \(r_{\perp i}\) is each mass's perpendicular distance from the axis. Moving two 1 kg masses from 0.5 m to 1 m from the same axis increases \(I\) from 0.5 to 2 kg m² without changing total mass. Fixed-axis rotational energy is \(K_{\mathrm{rot}}=\tfrac12I\omega^2\). In three dimensions, the inertia tensor is a matrix connecting angular-velocity components to angular-momentum components; it accounts for axis dependence and coupling between directions, so the two vectors need not be parallel.

Consider a uniform solid cylinder of mass \(m\), radius \(R\), and \(I_{\mathrm{CM}}=\tfrac12mR^2\), released from rest down a fixed incline through height \(h=1\) m. Assume sufficient static friction for no slip, no deformation, no rolling resistance, and negligible air drag. The contact point is instantaneously at rest, so ideal static friction does no work, although it supplies torque. Energy gives \(mgh=\tfrac12mv_{\mathrm{CM}}^2+\tfrac12I_{\mathrm{CM}}\omega^2=\tfrac34mv_{\mathrm{CM}}^2\), using \(v_{\mathrm{CM}}=R\omega\). Thus \(v_{\mathrm{CM}}=\sqrt{4gh/3}\approx3.62\) m/s. The friction is generally nonzero here; slipping or a moving contact surface changes the work and constraints.

Scope and Limits

The angular-momentum formula is for a particle; sum contributions for a system. Rotational energy assumes rigid rotation. Rolling also has translational energy.
