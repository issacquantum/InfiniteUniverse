Unitary Gate Condition

Equation

$$ U^\dagger U = I $$

Literal Reading

U dagger U equals I.

Common Reading

An ideal quantum gate preserves inner products, norms, and total probability; it can change individual amplitudes.

Symbols

\(U\): the quantum gate or circuit operation.

\(U^\dagger\): the conjugate transpose of \(U\), which reverses the operation for a unitary gate.

\(I\): the identity operator, which leaves every state unchanged.

Meaning of the Equation

A unitary gate is reversible and preserves total probability. That is why ideal closed-system quantum circuits evolve amplitudes without losing normalization.

Conditions and units

For a square finite-dimensional matrix, the displayed relation also implies \(UU^\dagger=I\), so \(U^{-1}=U^\dagger\). All entries are dimensionless. For a map between different dimensions, \(U^\dagger U=I\) states only an isometry; in infinite dimensions surjectivity must also be checked. Noisy channels and selected measurement outcomes are not generally unitary gates.

Example: a phase gate changes interference without losing probability

What does a probability-preserving gate actually do to a qubit? Let \(|0\rangle\) and \(|1\rangle\) be two normalized, distinguishable basis states. A phase gate leaves the amplitude of \(|0\rangle\) unchanged and multiplies the amplitude of \(|1\rangle\) by \(i\), the imaginary unit. In this basis, its diagonal entries are 1 and \(i\); the zero off-diagonal entries mean that it does not mix the basis states.

An equal superposition changes from \((|0\rangle+|1\rangle)/\sqrt{2}\) to \((|0\rangle+i|1\rangle)/\sqrt{2}\). Both basis measurements still have probability one half because multiplying by \(i\) changes phase, not magnitude. But a measurement in the plus/minus superposition basis changes from a certain plus result to equal probabilities: interference depends on that relative phase.

The adjoint reverses the phase multiplication, replacing \(i\) with \(-i\). Applying the gate and then its adjoint multiplies the second amplitude by \((-i)i=1\), and leaves the first unchanged. Thus their product is the identity \(I\), which leaves every input state unchanged. The example concerns an ideal isolated gate, not a noisy physical implementation.
