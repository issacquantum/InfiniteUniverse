Conditional Probability and Bayes

Equation

$$P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}=\frac{P(B\mid A)P(A)}{P(B)}$$

Literal Reading

P of A given B equals the intersection probability divided by P of B, also equal to P of B given A times P of A divided by P of B.

Common Reading

Update a probability using observed evidence.

Symbols

A, B: events; P(A): prior; P(B|A): likelihood; P(A|B): posterior.

Meaning of the Equation

P(B) must be positive; the expression P(B|A) also requires P(A)>0. Generalizations use densities or conditional distributions.

Conditions and units

\(\cap\) denotes intersection and \(P(A\cap B)\) the joint probability; \(P(B)\) is the evidence probability. All probabilities are dimensionless. This is an exact identity on one probability space, not a guarantee that an empirical likelihood model is correct.

Example: what a positive inspection result tells us

How often is a device faulty after it fails an inspection? Consider an illustrative batch of 100 devices. Let A mean that a device is faulty and B that the inspection flags it. Suppose 20 devices are faulty, 12 of those are flagged, and 30 devices in total are flagged. The inspection is imperfect: 18 flagged devices are not faulty.

Among the 30 flagged devices, 12 are faulty. The conditional probability \(P(A\mid B)\) is therefore 12 divided by 30, or 0.4. The prior probability \(P(A)\), before seeing the inspection result, is 20 divided by 100, or 0.2. The likelihood \(P(B\mid A)\) is 12 divided by 20, or 0.6; it asks how often the test flags a device known to be faulty.

Bayes’ rule gives the same answer: multiply 0.6 by 0.2 to obtain the joint probability 0.12, then divide by the overall flag probability \(P(B)=0.3\) to obtain 0.4. This posterior probability is not the likelihood 0.6: the two condition on different information. The figures are assumed counts, not measured performance of a real inspection system.
