Planck Blackbody Law

Equation

$$ B_\nu(\nu,T)=\frac{2h\nu^3}{c^2}\frac{1}{e^{h\nu/(k_BT)}-1} $$

Literal Reading

B subscript nu of nu and T equals two h nu cubed divided by c squared, multiplied by the reciprocal of the quantity e raised to h nu divided by k subscript B times T, minus one.

Common Reading

This is the frequency form of Planck’s blackbody law.

Symbols

\(B_\nu(\nu,T)\): spectral radiance per unit frequency.

\(h\): Planck's constant.

\(\nu\): the radiation frequency.

\(c\): the speed of light.

\(k_B\): Boltzmann's constant.

\(e^{h\nu/(k_B T)}\): the exponential factor that suppresses high-frequency emission.

Meaning of the Equation

The frequency label \(B_\nu\) must not be mixed with the wavelength form \(B_\lambda\), which has a different formula.

Conditions and units

\(T>0\) is absolute temperature in K and \(\nu>0\) frequency in Hz. In SI, \(B_\nu\) is in W m⁻² sr⁻¹ Hz⁻¹, \(h\) in J s, \(k_B\) in J/K, and \(c\) in m/s. The exponent is dimensionless and the minus one lies outside the exponential. This is equilibrium blackbody radiance in vacuum, not the total emitted flux; hemispheric integration gives \(\pi B_\nu\). Conversion to wavelength density requires the Jacobian \(|d\nu/d\lambda|\), not just substituting \(\nu=c/\lambda\).

Worked example or interpretation

When hν/(kBT)=1, the occupation factor is 1/(e−1)≈0.582. The classical low-frequency approximation would replace this factor by 1 at that point, so it already overestimates the radiance. Use h=6.62607015 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s, kB=1.380649 × 10⁻²³ J/K and c=299792458 m/s, all exact in SI.
