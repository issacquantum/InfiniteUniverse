Proper Time

Equation

$$d\tau = \frac{1}{c}\sqrt{-ds^2}$$

Literal Reading

d tau equals one over c times the square root of minus d s squared.

Common Reading

Proper time is the time measured along one worldline.

An ideal clock is a theoretical local clock that keeps perfect time along its own worldline and does not disturb the system being measured. It represents the clean limit of a real clock, not a magical device or a particular clock design.

Symbols

\(d\tau\): infinitesimal proper time.

\(ds^2\): spacetime interval using the \((-+++)\) sign convention.

\(c\): speed of light.

Meaning of the Equation

For signature \((-+++)\), timelike paths satisfy \(d\tau=\frac{1}{c}\sqrt{-ds^2}\). Other signature conventions reverse the sign inside the square root.

Conditions and units

For a future-directed timelike path, \(ds^2<0\), and the positive root gives elapsed proper time in seconds when \(ds^2\) is in m² and \(c\) in m/s. Integrating along the path gives a clock’s elapsed time. A null curve has zero proper-time interval and no rest-frame clock; a spacelike curve is not a physical clock worldline. Acceleration does not invalidate the local geometric definition.

Worked example or interpretation

For a timelike interval ds²=−9 m², the elapsed proper time is 3/c seconds, approximately 10.007 ns, using c=299792458 m/s. The minus sign inside the square root makes the result real for this signature.

Source

Sean M. Carroll, Lecture Notes on General Relativity.
