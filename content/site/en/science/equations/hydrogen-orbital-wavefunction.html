Hydrogen Orbital Wavefunction

Equation

$$ \psi_{n\ell m}(r, \theta, \phi) = R_{n\ell}(r) \cdot Y_\ell^m(\theta, \phi) $$

Literal Reading

Psi subscript n ell m of r, theta and phi equals R subscript n ell of r times Y subscript ell superscript m of theta and phi; m is an index, not a power.

Common Reading

The hydrogen orbital separates into a radial part and an angular part.

Symbols

\(\psi_{n\ell m}\): the full hydrogen eigenfunction labeled by three quantum numbers.

\(R_{n\ell}(r)\): the radial function, which controls how the state changes with distance from the nucleus.

\(Y_\ell^m(\theta,\phi)\): the spherical harmonic, which controls the angular shape of the orbital.

\(n,\ell,m\): the principal, orbital, and magnetic quantum numbers.

Meaning of the Equation

The separation shows why hydrogen orbitals have radial structure and angular shapes. The orbital picture comes from probability amplitudes, not from a tiny planet orbiting the nucleus.

Conditions and units

The spherical coordinates are radius \(r\ge0\), polar angle \(0\le\theta\le\pi\), and azimuth \(0\le\phi<2\pi\). Bound states have integer \(n\ge1\), \(0\le\ell\le n-1\), and \(-\ell\le m\le\ell\); this \(m\) is not a mass. With spherical harmonics normalized over solid angle, \(Y\) is dimensionless and both \(R\) and \(\psi\) have units m⁻³ᐟ² when \(r\) is in meters. The radial normalization uses \(r^2dr\). This separation applies to the nonrelativistic central Coulomb problem; electron spin is not included in the spatial function.

Worked example or interpretation

For n=1, ℓ=0 and m=0, the spherical harmonic is the constant 1/√(4π). The angular probability density is therefore isotropic. For higher ℓ, angular nodes arise from the spherical harmonic rather than from a classical orbit.
