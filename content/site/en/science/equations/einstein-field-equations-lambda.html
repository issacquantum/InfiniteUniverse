Einstein Field Equations with Lambda

Equation

$$G_{\mu\nu}+\Lambda g_{\mu\nu}=\frac{8\pi G}{c^4}T_{\mu\nu}$$

Literal Reading

G subscript mu nu plus capital lambda times g subscript mu nu equals eight pi G divided by c to the fourth, times T subscript mu nu.

Common Reading

Spacetime curvature is related to the stress-energy content of matter and radiation.

Symbols

\(G_{\mu\nu}\): Einstein tensor, encoding curvature.

\(\Lambda\): cosmological constant.

\(g_{\mu\nu}\): metric tensor.

\(T_{\mu\nu}\): stress energy tensor.

Meaning of the Equation

\(\Lambda\) acts as a cosmological-constant term. Moving it to the stress-energy side changes interpretation but not the equation’s content.

Conditions and units

Use signature \((-+++)\), coordinates \(x^0=ct\) with all coordinates in meters, and curvature \(R^\rho{}_{\sigma\mu\nu}=\partial_\mu\Gamma^\rho_{\nu\sigma}-\partial_\nu\Gamma^\rho_{\mu\sigma}+\Gamma^\rho_{\mu\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda_{\nu\sigma}-\Gamma^\rho_{\nu\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda_{\mu\sigma}\), with \(R_{\sigma\nu}=R^\rho{}_{\sigma\rho\nu}\). Greek indices run from 0 to 3. \(G_{\mu\nu}=R_{\mu\nu}-Rg_{\mu\nu}/2\), where \(R\) is scalar curvature; it has units m⁻². The metric is dimensionless and stress-energy has units J/m³ in this coordinate convention. The scalar \(G\) is Newton’s constant (m³ kg⁻¹ s⁻²), distinct from the Einstein tensor, and \(c\) is light speed (m/s). These are classical field equations, requiring matter equations and initial or boundary data; they are not a quantum-gravity law.

Conditions and units

\(\Lambda\) has units m⁻². The stress-energy tensor on the right excludes the vacuum contribution already represented by \(\Lambda\), avoiding double counting.

Worked example or interpretation

In vacuum with nonzero Λ, the equation gives Gμν=−Λgμν rather than zero. Contracting in four dimensions gives scalar curvature R=4Λ with the stated conventions. Λ is a model or observational parameter, not a universal value supplied by this equation; G≈6.67430 × 10⁻¹¹ m³ kg⁻¹ s⁻² and c=299792458 m/s.

Source

Sean M. Carroll, Lecture Notes on General Relativity.
