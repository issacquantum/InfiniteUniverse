Kepler Period for Two Bodies

Equation

$$T_{\mathrm{orb}}^2=\frac{4\pi^2a^3}{G(M+m)}$$

Literal reading

Orbital period squared equals four pi squared times a cubed, divided by G times the sum of M and m.

Conceptual reading

The total mass and relative orbit’s semimajor axis determine a bound orbit’s period.

Symbols

\(T_{\mathrm{orb}}\) is the period; orb labels orbital. \(a\) is the semimajor axis of the relative ellipse, half its longest diameter; \(M,m\) are masses; \(G\) is the gravitational constant; \(\pi\) is the dimensionless circle constant, approximately 3.14159. Exponents square period and pi and cube a; parentheses add masses before multiplying by G.

Values, units and domains

G = 6.67430(15) × 10⁻¹¹ m³·kg⁻¹·s⁻²; parentheses give the standard uncertainty in the last digits. Period is in s, a in m and masses in kg; the fraction has units s². Require positive masses and a.

Meaning

The relative separation vector traces an ellipse with a focus at the force centre. The relative semimajor axis differs from each body’s semimajor axis around the common centre of mass.

Assumptions and limits

Isolated Newtonian two-body system with a bound noncollision orbit. Third-body perturbations and relativistic corrections are excluded.

Example

With G(M+m) = 3.986 × 10¹⁴ m³/s² and a = 10⁷ m, the period is about 9952 s or 166 min. An eccentricity of 0.30 gives nearest and farthest separations 7 × 10⁶ m and 1.3 × 10⁷ m; it does not change the period at fixed a.

Source

Newton, Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica, Book I; NIST, CODATA 2022.

Orbital geometry and derivation

Kepler's first law: a bound, noncollision relative orbit is an ellipse with the force center at a focus, not generally at the ellipse's geometric center. The semimajor axis \(a\) is half its longest diameter; eccentricity \(e\) measures elongation, with \(0\le e<1\) and \(e=0\) a circle. The focus lies \(ae\) from the center, giving nearest and farthest separations \(a(1-e)\) and \(a(1+e)\). Second law: the line joining the bodies sweeps equal areas in equal times, so they move faster near closest approach. Third law: \(T_{\mathrm{orb}}^2=4\pi^2a^3/[G(M+m)]\). Here \(T_{\mathrm{orb}}\) is the period in seconds, not kinetic energy, and \(a\) is the semimajor axis of the relative orbit, not either body's individual orbit about the common center of mass.

As an elliptical example with negligible satellite mass, take Earth's \(GM=3.986\times10^{14}\) m³/s², \(a=1.0\times10^7\) m and \(e=0.30\). The nearest separation is \(7.0\times10^6\) m, the farthest \(1.3\times10^7\) m, and the period is approximately 9952 s, or 166 minutes. Taking Earth's radius as \(6.37\times10^6\) m, the nearest altitude is approximately 630 km, not 7000 km. Escape speed at that closest separation is about 10.67 km/s. Altitude is center-to-center distance minus the central body's radius.
