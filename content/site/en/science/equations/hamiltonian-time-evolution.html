Hamiltonian Time Evolution

Equation

$$ U(t)=e^{-iHt/\hbar} $$

Literal Reading

U of t equals the operator exponential of minus i times H times t divided by h-bar.

Common Reading

The rule that the Hamiltonian generates unitary time evolution for a closed quantum system.

Symbols

\(U(t)\): unitary time-evolution operator.

\(H\): Hamiltonian, the energy and interaction operator.

\(t\): elapsed time.

\(i\): imaginary unit.

\(\hbar\): reduced Planck constant.

Meaning of the Equation

This exponential applies to a closed system with a time-independent Hamiltonian \(H\), with evolution starting at time zero. For a general time-dependent Hamiltonian the propagator requires a time-ordered exponential; simply substituting \(H(t)\) into this expression is not valid.

Units and scope

\(U\) is dimensionless; \(H\) has energy units (J), \(t\) is in seconds and \(\hbar\) in J s, making the exponent dimensionless. \(i^2=-1\). Self-adjointness of \(H\) gives unitary evolution. The Hamiltonian and state depend on the system; this formula does not supply their numerical values.

Sources

MIT, Quantum Physics I, lecture notes (publication page).

Example: how the relative phase of two states changes

Can a state change even when an energy measurement keeps the same probabilities? Consider a closed system with two distinguishable energy states. The symbols \(|0\rangle\) and \(|1\rangle\), called kets, label these states; the numbers are labels, not probabilities. They are normalized and orthogonal: each has total probability one, and an energy measurement can distinguish them perfectly.

Choose the energy reference so that \(|0\rangle\) has energy zero and \(|1\rangle\) has energy \(\Delta\), where \(\Delta>0\) is the energy difference in joules. The Hamiltonian \(H\) is the operator representing energy. In this energy-state basis, it multiplies the first state by zero and the second by \(\Delta\), without mixing them. That is what diagonal means here: a matrix written using these two states has its energy values on the main diagonal and zeros elsewhere.

Let \(t\) be elapsed time in seconds, \(i\) the imaginary unit with \(i^2=-1\), and \(\hbar\) the reduced Planck constant. Its units are joule-seconds. Dividing an energy by \(\hbar\) gives the angular rate at which its complex amplitude rotates in phase; thus \(\Delta t/\hbar\) is a dimensionless angle in radians. The constant links energy to phase evolution, rather than serving only as a numerical conversion.

Write the state as \(|\psi(t)\rangle\). Prepare an equal coherent superposition at time zero:

$$|\psi(0)\rangle=\frac{|0\rangle+|1\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}.$$

The evolution operator multiplies each energy component by \(e^{-iEt/\hbar}\), where \(E\) is that component’s energy and \(e\) is the exponential base. The zero-energy component gets the factor one. The other gets \(e^{-i\Delta t/\hbar}\). Therefore:

$$|\psi(t)\rangle=\frac{|0\rangle+e^{-i\Delta t/\hbar}|1\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}.$$

At \(t=\pi\hbar/\Delta\), the angle is \(\pi\). A half-turn in the complex plane gives \(e^{-i\pi}=-1\), so:

$$|\psi(\pi\hbar/\Delta)\rangle=\frac{|0\rangle-|1\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}.$$

Relative phase means the difference between the phases of the two amplitudes. It changes from zero to \(\pi\). Their magnitudes remain \(1/\sqrt{2}\), so an energy measurement still gives each energy with probability \(1/2\). Those probabilities add to one, which is why the state remains normalized. The mean energy also remains \(\Delta/2\).

The phase change is nevertheless measurable in another basis. A measurement that distinguishes the plus superposition \((|0\rangle+|1\rangle)/\sqrt{2}\) from the minus superposition \((|0\rangle-|1\rangle)/\sqrt{2}\) gives the plus result with certainty initially and the minus result with certainty at the specified time. Interference between amplitudes distinguishes the two states. This is a relative phase change, not an unobservable common phase multiplying the whole state.

The mathematical reason is that applying a function of a time-independent Hamiltonian to an energy eigenstate applies that function to its energy eigenvalue. The example assumes isolated evolution with no driving that mixes these levels. Environmental decoherence or a time-dependent interaction can change the result.
