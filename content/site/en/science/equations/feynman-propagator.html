Feynman Propagator

Equation

$$D_F(x-y)=\langle 0|T\{\phi(x)\phi(y)\}|0\rangle$$

Literal Reading

D sub F of x minus y equals vacuum expectation value of the time-ordered product phi of x phi of y.

Common Reading

The Feynman propagator is the vacuum expectation value of the time-ordered product and gives the amplitude used for propagation or internal lines in perturbation theory.

Symbols

\(D_F\): Feynman propagator.

\(T\): time ordering operator.

\(|0\rangle\): vacuum state.

Meaning of the Equation

It is not a simple picture of a field influence traveling like a classical signal; it is a covariant quantum object used inside amplitude calculations.

Form used in the article

$$D_F(x-y)=\int \frac{d^4p}{(2\pi)^4}\frac{i e^{-ip\cdot(x-y)}}{p^2-m^2+i\epsilon}$$

D subscript F of x minus y equals the integral over four-momentum, with measure d four p divided by two pi to the fourth, of i times e raised to minus i p dot x minus y, divided by p squared minus m squared plus i epsilon.

This Fourier form is for a free real scalar field in four-dimensional Minkowski spacetime with signature (+,−,−,−) and \(\hbar=c=1\). \(x,y\) are events, \(p\) is four-momentum, \(m\) is the field mass, \(i^2=-1\), and \(\epsilon\to0^+\) specifies the Feynman pole prescription. Momentum and mass have energy units; coordinates have inverse-energy units and the propagator has energy-squared units. The integration covers all four-momenta, not only the mass shell. The field \(\phi\) and time ordering in the first formula define the same two-point function. David Tong, Quantum Field Theory, Free Fields.

Worked example or interpretation

If x occurs later than y, time ordering leaves φ(x)φ(y) in that order; if y is later, it gives φ(y)φ(x) for this bosonic field. Thus the propagator differs from a fixed-order Wightman function. Neither nonzero value at spacelike separation is a controllable signal.
