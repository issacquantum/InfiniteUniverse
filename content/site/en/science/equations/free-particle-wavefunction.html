Free Particle Wavefunction

Equation

$$ \psi(x,t)=Ae^{i(kx-\omega t)} $$

Literal Reading

Psi of x and t equals A times e raised to i times the entire quantity k x minus omega t.

Common Reading

A time-dependent plane-wave solution for a free particle.

Symbols

\(\psi(x,t)\): the particle's wavefunction at position \(x\) and time \(t\).

\(A\): the amplitude of the wave.

\(k\): wave number, related to momentum.

\(\omega\): angular frequency, related to energy.

Meaning of the Equation

This is an ideal plane-wave momentum eigenstate and is not normalizable over all space; finite periodic normalization is an idealization, while a wave packet is a different state built from multiple plane waves.

Form used in the article

$$\psi(\mathbf{r}) = Ae^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}}$$

Psi of vector r equals A times e raised to i times the dot product of vector k and vector r.

This is the spatial part of the three-dimensional plane wave. \(\mathbf r\) is position (m), \(\mathbf k\) is wave vector (m⁻¹), \(i\) is the imaginary unit, and \(A\) is an amplitude fixed by normalization convention. For box normalization, \(A\) and \(\psi\) have units \(\mathrm{m}^{-3/2}\). Including time multiplies it by \(e^{-i\omega t}\), with angular frequency \(\omega\) in s⁻¹ and \(t\) in seconds. For a nonrelativistic free particle of mass \(m\), \(\hbar\omega=\hbar^2|\mathbf k|^2/(2m)\). A localized particle requires a superposition of plane waves.

Conditions and units

For the one-dimensional formula, \(x\) is in m, \(t\) in s, \(k\) in m⁻¹, and \(\omega\) in s⁻¹. With box length \(L\), a normalized plane wave has \(|A|=L^{-1/2}\), in m⁻¹ᐟ². Its momentum is \(\hbar k\), its kinetic energy \(\hbar\omega=\hbar^2k^2/(2m)\), and \(m\) is the particle mass. A wave packet is a superposition, not a normalization of a single infinite plane wave.

Example: uniform position probability on a ring

How can a wave have a changing phase but a constant position probability? Use a one-dimensional interval of length \(L\) whose ends are identified, as on an ideal ring. This is a periodic boundary condition, not a box with impenetrable walls. One full phase cycle around the ring gives \(k=2\pi/L\). Choose the amplitude \(A=1/\sqrt{L}\).

The complex exponential has magnitude one, so the probability density \(|\psi(x,t)|^2\) is \(1/L\) everywhere. Density means probability per unit length: an interval of length \(L/2\) contains the particle with probability one half. Integrating over the whole length gives one, which normalizes the state. Here \(e\) is the exponential base and \(i^2=-1\).

The state has definite momentum \(p=\hbar k\), where \(p\) is momentum and \(\hbar\) is the reduced Planck constant in joule-seconds. It does not describe a particle localized at one point. A localized state requires a wave packet, a superposition of different wave numbers. A single plane wave on the entire infinite line cannot be normalized to total probability one.
