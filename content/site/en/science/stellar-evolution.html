Energy, Evolution, and Stellar Endings

Fusion and the stellar thermostat

Nuclear fusion changes nuclei and releases energy when the final products are more tightly bound overall. Thermal collisions and quantum tunneling let some nuclei cross the effective Coulomb barrier. Stellar hydrogen burning converts hydrogen into helium through reaction networks, not a chemical flame. Neutrinos carry away part of the energy, so not all reaction energy heats the gas.

The proton-proton chain dominates hydrogen burning in Sun-like conditions. Its slow weak-interaction step helps set the rate. The CNO cycle uses carbon, nitrogen, and oxygen nuclei as catalysts and becomes increasingly important at higher core temperatures, provided those nuclei are available. Its temperature sensitivity is stronger in the relevant regime. The crossover depends on composition and conditions, not a universal mass boundary.

In a nondegenerate core, extra heating can cause expansion, reducing temperature and reaction rates. This feedback helps stabilize main-sequence burning. Degenerate matter has a much weaker thermal pressure response, so heating may fail to expand it promptly and a runaway can occur. This does not mean every degenerate object is exploding. Reaction rates, cooling, transport, and expansion times must be compared.

Helium burning combines helium nuclei through the triple-alpha process to make carbon; subsequent alpha capture can make oxygen. The carbon-to-oxygen balance depends on competing reaction rates and mixing. Massive stars can reach carbon, neon, oxygen, and silicon burning, with networks and photodisintegration reorganizing nuclei. Fusion beyond the iron-group binding-energy peak cannot provide the same sustained net energy source.

Different masses, different histories

Low-mass red dwarfs consume fuel slowly; the least massive hydrogen-burning stars have predicted lifetimes longer than the current age of the Universe. Their completed isolated evolution is therefore a model prediction, not an observed population of ancient final states. Deep convection can mix fuel through much of a small star, changing how much hydrogen is accessible.

For a Sun-like star, exhaustion of central hydrogen leaves a helium-rich core, hydrogen burning in a shell, and an expanding giant envelope. Core contraction and shell heating do not contradict envelope expansion. Helium ignition can be rapid in a degenerate core, but the helium flash in an ordinary low-mass giant does not blow apart the entire star. Later helium and hydrogen shell burning, thermal pulses, mixing, and winds reshape the star and its surroundings.

A lost envelope can be illuminated by the hot exposed core as a planetary nebula; the name does not mean it forms planets, and not every low- or intermediate-mass star produces a conspicuous visible nebula. The remaining white dwarf cools while electron degeneracy provides most of its support. Cooling includes heat capacity, envelope insulation, crystallization, and composition separation, so luminosity is not a simple universal age clock.

Massive stars generally radiate so much more intensely that their greater fuel supply lasts less time. Late burning stages can become very short because neutrino losses are large. Winds, metallicity, rotation, mixing, and binary interaction change core mass and envelope structure. Initial mass alone does not provide an exact, universal boundary between white dwarf, neutron star, and black hole outcomes.

Collapse and thermonuclear disruption

In many massive-star cores, electron captures reduce electron pressure and photodisintegration absorbs energy as collapse develops. Matter reaching nuclear density stiffens, launching a shock that can stall as it loses energy. Neutrino heating, turbulent motions, accretion, and sometimes rotation and magnetic fields affect whether an explosion succeeds. A core-collapse supernova can leave a neutron star or a black hole; some collapse events are weak or fail to expel much material. The mapping from progenitor to outcome is an active problem.

A Type Ia supernova instead involves thermonuclear burning of a white dwarf in a binary history, often a carbon-oxygen white dwarf. Accretion, mergers, and helium-triggered detonations provide different possible channels. The event is not simply a miniature version of massive-star core collapse, nor does every case require reaching one exact limiting mass. Spectral classes describe observations; the physical interpretation requires models and multiple diagnostics.

Mass transfer begins when material can flow through the binary's gravitational geometry. It can strip an envelope, increase a companion's mass, change rotation, or produce a common-envelope phase in which both cores orbit inside shared gas. Mergers can create stars whose apparent evolutionary state no longer matches a single-star age estimate. Binary evolution is central to many remnant and transient populations, not a small correction applied after every single-star calculation.

Elements, winds, and magnetic activity

Nucleosynthesis is not one process that makes every heavy element in an ordinary stellar core. Hydrogen and much helium have primordial origins. Hydrostatic and explosive burning produce different nuclei; slow neutron capture in evolved stars and rapid neutron capture in neutron-rich outflows populate different isotopes. Neutron-star mergers are an established site of rapid neutron-capture production, but their contribution through Galactic history and the roles of other sites remain under study. Some light nuclei are importantly produced by cosmic-ray fragmentation. An element's origin can be distributed among several channels.

Stellar winds and explosions return material to interstellar gas. Yield predictions depend on what is synthesized and what actually escapes rather than falling into a remnant. Spectra of stars of different ages and compositions, meteorite isotope measurements, supernova light curves, and merger observations test different parts of this picture.

Rotation and convection can sustain magnetic activity. Dark spots alter light curves as a star rotates; flares release magnetic energy, and winds carry mass and angular momentum. Magnetic braking can slow rotation, but its efficiency depends on stellar properties and evolutionary state. Rotation-age relations require calibration and do not apply unchanged to every star. Activity affects nearby planetary atmospheres and can imitate or obscure small oscillation and transit signals. Magnetic fields also influence mixing and transport inside stars, where their geometry is difficult to measure directly.
