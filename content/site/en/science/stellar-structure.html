Stellar Structure and Hydrostatic Equilibrium

A pressure difference supports each layer

Consider a nearly spherical star whose material has negligible bulk acceleration. Gravity pulls each small parcel inward. Pressure acts on every face of the parcel, not in one preferred outward direction. Because pressure is larger on the inner face than on the outer face, the net pressure force points outward. Hydrostatic equilibrium is a local force balance, not a statement that gravity has disappeared or that the star has stopped evolving.

Take a narrow radial column through a thin spherical layer. Its cross-sectional area is A, its thickness dr, and its mass is approximately density times A times dr. To leading order, the radial pressure force is the inner pressure minus the outer pressure, multiplied by A. The gravitational force has magnitude G times enclosed mass times the column's mass divided by radius squared. Set the sum to zero, divide by A and dr, and take the thin-layer limit. The same result follows from the radial component of the fluid momentum equation. The shell theorem supplies the enclosed mass for spherical Newtonian gravity.

Here P is total isotropic pressure in Pa, r is radius in m, M(r) is enclosed mass in kg, density is rho in kg/m³, and G has units m³ kg⁻¹ s⁻². The gradient is in Pa/m. Its negative sign means pressure decreases outward. The equation breakdown includes a numerical example for a stellar layer. At the center, enclosed mass vanishes rapidly enough that the regular pressure gradient tends to zero; the apparent radius denominator is not a physical central singularity.

Mass and pressure must agree

A shell's volume is its spherical area times its thickness to leading order. Multiplying by local density gives the mass added when the radius increases. This supplies the enclosed mass required in the force-balance equation.

The two radial equations couple density, enclosed mass, and pressure. Choosing a pressure profile independently of the density profile will generally violate one of them. A uniform-density sphere is a useful classroom model, but the Sun is strongly centrally concentrated. Neither equation specifies temperature or composition. They cannot decide whether an object is a protostar, a main-sequence star, or a cooling remnant without further physics.

Worked model: mass determines the pressure profile

Choose a spherical model with constant density rho₀=1,000 kg/m³, outer radius R=1.0 × 10⁹ m, no central point mass, and surface pressure P(R)=0. These are deliberately imposed assumptions, not measurements of a particular star. Follow the same material from its enclosed mass to its pressure profile.

Start with the mass-continuity equation. Replace density by the constant rho₀ and integrate from the center to r. The integral of radius squared is radius cubed divided by three. The central condition M(0)=0 removes the integration constant.

With rho₀=1,000 kg/m³ and R=1.0 × 10⁹ m, total mass is about 4.189 × 10³⁰ kg. At r=R/2 it is 5.236 × 10²⁹ kg. The next calculation uses that interior mass, not the full mass, for local gravity.

Substitute the integrated uniform-density mass into Newtonian hydrostatic balance. One factor of density comes from the enclosed mass and the other from the weight of the local material. Cancel two powers of radius. This is the explicit connection between the mass and pressure equations.

At rho₀=1,000 kg/m³, R=1.0 × 10⁹ m and r=R/2, the gradient is about −1.398 × 10⁵ Pa/m. Across 1 km it produces a pressure drop of about 1.398 × 10⁸ Pa if the gradient is treated as locally constant.

Integrate the uniform-density pressure gradient from r to R. The integral of radius is half the difference of the squared limits. Apply P(R)=0 and reverse the sign to obtain the positive interior pressure. Differentiating the result recovers the local gradient, while evaluating it at R checks the surface condition.

For rho₀=1,000 kg/m³ and R=1.0 × 10⁹ m, central pressure is about 1.398 × 10¹⁴ Pa. At half the radius it is three quarters of that value, about 1.048 × 10¹⁴ Pa. The surface value is zero by construction, not because every real photosphere has zero pressure.

Uniform density, Newtonian spherical hydrostatic balance and zero external pressure. A nonzero imposed surface pressure would be added everywhere. This mechanical model does not solve temperature, opacity, fusion, transport or stability and is not a self-consistent model of a real gas star.

An equation of state is not a fuel supply

An equation of state relates pressure to density, temperature, and composition. For a dilute, nondegenerate ideal mixture, gas pressure follows the particle number density and temperature. Electrons count as particles as well as ions, so ionization changes the mean mass per freely moving particle.

In a sufficiently opaque region close to local thermodynamic equilibrium, radiation also contributes isotropic pressure. This expression does not describe a freely streaming beam or an optically thin atmosphere.

In dense matter, electrons can be degenerate: quantum states fill up to high momenta even at low thermal temperature. Compression raises their characteristic momenta and pressure. Nonrelativistic degenerate electron pressure increases roughly as density to the five-thirds power at fixed composition; at relativistic momenta the dependence softens toward the four-thirds power. These are limiting behaviors, not a complete equation of state.

A white dwarf is supported largely by electron degeneracy while its stored thermal energy slowly escapes. Fusion is not required for that support. Its limiting mass depends on the relativistic response, composition, and additional effects; an ideal nonrotating limit is not a universal explosion trigger. Neutron-star matter needs a different, uncertain high-density equation of state, including nuclear interactions. Degeneracy does not mean gravity can never win.

Mechanical balance is not thermal balance

A star can be mechanically balanced while losing stored energy and contracting slowly. Thermal balance requires accounting for local heating, neutrino losses, compression, and changes in internal energy. Let luminosity mean the outward energy flow through a sphere per unit time, not the energy stored inside it. A shell can increase or decrease that flow.

The energy equation is written following a mass shell, with nuclear heating and neutrino losses per unit mass. Internal energy and pressure-volume work describe gravothermal changes. Nuclear binding-energy conventions must be handled consistently so that reaction energy is not counted twice. A steady main-sequence approximation neglects these secular thermal changes; an evolving protostar cannot generally do so. During rapid collapse, a quasistatic structure calculation must be replaced or supplemented by hydrodynamics.

Energy generated in the core does not instantly appear at the photosphere. In an opaque interior, photons are absorbed, emitted, and scattered. Radiative diffusion transports net energy down a temperature gradient, with opacity describing resistance to transport.

If radiation alone would require too steep a temperature gradient, a displaced fluid element can remain buoyant and convection transports enthalpy. Composition gradients also affect buoyancy. The actual convective gradient and mixing require a closure prescription, often a one-dimensional mixing-length approximation, rather than simply substituting the radiative gradient everywhere. Electron conduction matters in degenerate interiors; neutrinos can remove energy efficiently in late burning stages and compact remnants. These transport channels must enter the appropriate regime.

Solving a connected boundary-value problem

Hydrostatic balance, mass conservation, energy conservation, and energy transport jointly determine pressure, enclosed mass, luminosity, and temperature profiles. Density comes from the equation of state. Reaction rates, opacity, composition evolution, and mixing couple back into all four. Numerical evolution advances composition and thermal state in time while resolving spatial layers; it is not four independent curve fits.

At a regular center, enclosed mass and luminosity vanish and central pressure and temperature remain finite. At the outer boundary, an atmosphere model relates pressure and temperature to optical depth and escaping radiation. Setting surface pressure to zero is a simplification, not a realistic photospheric boundary in every calculation. A specified total mass and composition help select solutions, while evolutionary history supplies further constraints. Convergence with spatial and temporal resolution must be checked separately from agreement with observations.

The present Sun has a radiative interior beneath a convective envelope and gains most of its energy from core hydrogen fusion. A red giant can retain a compact contracting core while its extended envelope expands; there is no requirement that all radii move in the same direction. A protostar can radiate contraction energy, and a white dwarf can cool with degeneracy support. The same mechanical framework is useful in several of these cases, but their equations of state, energy terms, and boundary conditions differ.

Contraction, heating, and timescales

For an isolated, nonrotating, hydrostatic sphere dominated by a monatomic ideal gas, with negligible surface pressure and radiation energy, the scalar virial theorem connects internal thermal energy with negative gravitational potential energy.

When such a system loses total energy slowly and contracts through nearby equilibria, its potential energy becomes more negative and its internal thermal energy rises. Roughly half the released binding energy heats the gas and half is radiated in this restricted model. This is the negative heat-capacity behavior of a self-gravitating system, not a claim that each parcel has negative ordinary heat capacity. Degeneracy, radiation pressure, rotation, surface terms, and rapid motions change the argument.

The dynamical scale estimates how quickly gravity and pressure can rearrange the object. It is an order-of-magnitude scale, not the exact free-fall time for every density profile. For solar mass and radius it is about 1,600 seconds.

The Kelvin-Helmholtz estimate divides a gravitational binding-energy scale by luminosity: about 30 million years for present solar values, with a structure-dependent coefficient omitted. It shows why contraction alone cannot power the Sun for its inferred multibillion-year age.

The nuclear estimate accounts for the fraction of mass that can actually supply fuel, conversion efficiency, and luminosity. A simple solar estimate gives an order of ten billion years, not a universal stellar lifetime. In a stable main-sequence model, dynamical adjustment is much faster than thermal evolution, which is faster than core fuel exhaustion. Explosive burning or collapse can reverse the useful ordering and invalidate quasistatic assumptions.

Equilibrium, stability, and relativity

Equilibrium asks whether forces balance in a reference configuration; stability asks how a disturbance evolves. A compressed layer may rebound, oscillate with damping, grow unstable, or settle into a new configuration. Heat exchange during a pulsation can drive it instead of damping it. A temporary imbalance does not imply destruction. Sustained loss of support or sufficiently rapid energy release can instead produce collapse or explosion.

Rapid rotation adds centrifugal effects and distorts the star. Magnetic tension is directional, and tides from a companion break spherical symmetry. A one-dimensional radial model can sometimes use averaged corrections, but cannot represent every latitude, field geometry, or tidal flow. These are limits of the model, not exceptions to conservation laws.

For very compact objects, gravity responds to energy and pressure and spacetime curvature changes the balance. The Tolman-Oppenheimer-Volkoff equation describes a static spherical relativistic fluid with isotropic pressure.

It reduces to Newtonian hydrostatic balance in the weak-field, low-pressure limit. Ordinary Sun-like stars do not require a full relativistic structure calculation to explain their main pressure balance. Neutron stars do. Their measured masses, radii, tidal responses, and oscillations constrain possible equations of state; a static solution need not be dynamically stable.

Stellar oscillations test the restoring forces and sound-speed structure around equilibrium. Frequencies sensitive to different depths can reject interior models that reproduce surface brightness alone. The asteroseismology chapter develops this observational test rather than treating equilibrium as an untestable assumption.
