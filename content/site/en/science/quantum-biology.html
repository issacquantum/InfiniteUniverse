Quantum Biology

Quantum biology studies how quantum processes contribute to biological mechanisms. All molecular biology rests on quantum chemistry, but a more demanding question is whether a particular effect, such as tunneling or spin coherence, changes a reaction rate, energy-transfer pathway, or sensory response in a living system. A molecular effect, its physiological role, and an evolutionary advantage are three different claims.

Molecular structure is the starting point

Electronic states determine the bonds, charge distributions, and interactions of proteins, pigments, and nucleic acids. These properties shape the energy landscape on which molecules move and react. Biological organization adds regulation, compartmentalization, feedback, and evolution. A calculation of one bond does not explain an organism, just as a cellular model does not replace the chemistry of that bond.

Many useful biological models treat nuclear positions, concentrations, or reaction networks classically while obtaining energies and rate parameters from quantum calculations or experiments. This is not a contradiction. The question is whether the approximation retains the observable being studied. If tunneling substantially affects a rate, treating the transferring particle as a classical object crossing a barrier may fail even when the surrounding protein is described approximately classically.

Warm environments, coherence, and timescales

A biomolecule is an open system: it exchanges energy and correlations with solvent, vibrations, and nearby molecules. Decoherence suppresses observable phase relations in a chosen subsystem; relaxation changes energy populations. Neither term means that quantum chemistry stops operating. Different electronic, vibrational, and spin degrees of freedom can have very different lifetimes in the same sample.

Functional relevance depends on competing timescales. A brief coherent motion can matter if a reaction uses it before it decays. Long-lived oscillations can be irrelevant if they do not affect the measured yield. A signal created by carefully timed laboratory laser pulses also need not occur in the same way under ordinary sunlight. Establishing coherence, identifying which states are coherent, and showing a biological benefit require separate evidence.

Vision: a photon starts a chemical change

In rhodopsin, a protein binds the light-sensitive retinal molecule. Absorption excites the chromophore, meaning the molecular group that absorbs the light, and changes the electronic energy landscape. Retinal can then change from its 11-cis configuration toward an all-trans configuration. Protein rearrangements initiate biochemical signaling that is amplified by the cell. The first photochemical step is quantum; the complete visual response spans molecular, cellular, and neural scales.

Ultrafast spectroscopy and time-resolved structural work probe this early reaction. Schoenlein et al. (1991) measured an approximately 200-femtosecond isomerization, while Ultrafast structural changes direct the first molecular events of vision (2023) resolved early structural changes. Neither result implies that conscious perception remains in a coherent superposition of visual outcomes.

Photosynthesis: absorption, transfer, and charge separation

A pigment absorbs a photon and becomes electronically excited. Coupling between pigments allows excitation energy to move toward a reaction center, where charge separation begins the conversion into chemical free energy. Excitation-energy transfer is not the same process as transferring an electron between distant molecules. An exciton describes an electronic excitation that may extend over coupled pigments.

The protein environment shifts energies, provides vibrations, and influences relaxation. Depending on coupling and environmental fluctuations, a useful model may emphasize delocalized excitonic states, transfer between localized states, or an intermediate regime. No general rule says that all environmental disturbance is harmful or that maximal coherence always maximizes useful energy transfer.

Engel et al. (2007) reported long-lived beating signals in a photosynthetic complex at low temperature and interpreted them in terms of electronic coherence. Later work distinguished electronic, vibrational, and mixed contributions. Duan et al. (2017) found rapid electronic decoherence under their conditions. These results do not justify saying that plants search all possible paths as a quantum computer.

The established foundation is quantum light absorption and molecular energy transfer. The extent to which particular coherences improve biological performance depends on the complex, conditions, and observable. Cao et al., Quantum biology revisited (2020) reviews why spectroscopic oscillations alone do not settle that functional question. High excitation-transfer yield must also be distinguished from the overall efficiency of converting sunlight into stored biomass.

Electron transfer in respiration and photosynthesis

Redox reactions transfer electrons between donors and acceptors. Protein-bound cofactors arrange such transfers into pathways that help convert energy in respiration and photosynthesis. Electron tunneling permits transfer through an intervening barrier without requiring a classical trajectory over it. Its probability depends strongly on electronic coupling and geometry.

Nuclear rearrangement and the surrounding electrostatic environment also matter: the donor, acceptor, protein, and solvent must accommodate the changed charge distribution. Distance alone does not determine the rate. Several short transfers through intermediate cofactors can differ greatly from one long transfer. Their direction and timing must be understood together with free-energy differences and competing reactions.

Moser et al., Nature of biological electron transfer (1992) and Page et al., Natural engineering principles of electron tunnelling in biological oxidation-reduction (1999) connect molecular organization with electron-transfer behavior. Tunneling in these processes does not imply coherent transport across an entire organism.

Hydrogen tunneling and enzyme catalysis

An enzyme changes the energetic and geometric conditions of a reaction. Hydrogen transfer can involve a proton, a hydrogen atom, or a hydride, depending on the chemistry. Because hydrogen nuclei are light, tunneling may contribute appreciably to some transfer steps. This is distinct from the electron tunneling described above; the moving degrees of freedom and their environment are different.

Replacing hydrogen with deuterium changes nuclear mass and vibrational energies. The resulting kinetic isotope effect, a change in reaction rate between isotopic variants, helps probe the mechanism. An isotope effect by itself is not proof of tunneling: zero-point-energy differences and other kinetic steps also matter. Temperature dependence, mutations, structural information, and competing kinetic models strengthen the inference.

Klinman and Kohen, Hydrogen Tunneling Links Protein Dynamics to Enzyme Catalysis (2013) examines evidence for hydrogen tunneling and the role of donor-acceptor configurations. A tunneling contribution does not establish that the enzyme evolved specifically to enhance tunneling, or that every catalytic reaction uses it as its dominant mechanism.

Magnetoreception and radical-pair chemistry

A radical pair contains two unpaired electrons whose spin states can be correlated. Interactions with nearby nuclear spins and an external magnetic field influence singlet-triplet evolution. If reaction channels depend on spin, that evolution can alter chemical product yields. A field need not supply the reaction's chemical energy to affect its branching probabilities.

Cryptochrome proteins are candidates for light-dependent magnetic sensing in birds. Xu et al. (2021) demonstrated magnetic sensitivity of European robin cryptochrome 4 in vitro. This supports a molecular candidate; it does not by itself identify the complete compass pathway in a living bird.

A biological account must connect molecular orientation, relevant field strengths, reaction lifetimes, signaling, and behavior. Experiments on purified protein, manipulated cells, and navigating animals address different parts of that chain. A result obtained for one species or preparation cannot automatically be generalized to every migratory animal. The proposed radical-pair mechanism remains a specific, testable hypothesis rather than a universal explanation of navigation.

Proton transfer in DNA: a mechanism under investigation

Moving protons along hydrogen bonds can change the tautomeric form of a base, meaning its arrangement of protons and bonds. A rare tautomer may present a different pairing pattern. If such a state persists during a relevant step in replication and escapes correction, it could contribute to a mutation. Formation of a tautomer is therefore not identical to a fixed genetic change.

An open quantum systems approach to proton tunnelling in DNA (2022) models how environmental coupling and tunneling affect proton-transfer populations. It is a theoretical investigation, not a measurement of the fraction of human mutations caused by tunneling. Solvent, sequence, enzymes, strand separation, lifetimes, and repair must be considered before translating a molecular calculation into a mutation rate.

DNA damage, replication errors, chemical modification, and repair already provide many established routes to genetic change. Quantum contributions to particular molecular steps should be evaluated within that biology, not used to replace it with a single explanation.

Olfaction and claims that need stronger evidence

One proposal assigns odor discrimination to electron transfer assisted by molecular vibrations. Isotopic substitution can test aspects of this idea, but it can also alter binding and other chemical properties. Behavioral discrimination and receptor activation are different measurements. Block et al. (2015) found results inconsistent with the vibrational mechanism in the receptors they tested. The proposal should not be presented as an established general theory of smell.

Nor do the mechanisms in this chapter establish quantum theories of consciousness or claims of quantum healing. Such claims need their own defined mechanisms, measurements, and reproducible evidence. Demonstrating molecular coherence in one preparation cannot be used as evidence for unrelated biological or metaphysical conclusions.

How a biological quantum claim is tested

Start with a measurable outcome: reaction rate, product yield, energy-transfer time, or controlled sensory response. Specify the preparation and conditions. Compare predictions with a model that lacks the proposed effect, without giving the competing model unrealistically poor parameters. Isotope substitution, magnetic fields, temperature changes, and targeted molecular changes can help isolate mechanisms, but each intervention also needs controls for ordinary chemical or structural changes.

The strongest argument connects a measured microscopic effect to a change in biological function and survives independent tests. Evidence for evolutionary selection requires a further comparison of biological performance and alternatives. This distinction allows quantum biology to be both ambitious and precise: well-established chemistry, active mechanistic research, and speculative extensions remain identifiable.
